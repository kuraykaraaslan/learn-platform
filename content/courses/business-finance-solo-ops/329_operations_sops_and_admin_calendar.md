# 329. Turning Recurring Admin Work into SOPs

## What It Is
A solo operator has no colleague to catch a forgotten renewal or a missed follow-up — memory is the only backstop, and memory is unreliable under delivery pressure. The fix is to convert recurring admin work into standard operating procedures (SOPs) tied to a fixed calendar, so obligations don't depend on remembering to remember them.

The areas that most benefit from an SOP are the ones that repeat with real consequences if skipped: new client onboarding, invoice issuing, payment follow-up, expense recording, subscription review, the monthly close, project financial close, accountant handoff, backing up finance records, client offboarding, and support request intake. Each SOP needs the same seven fields regardless of topic — purpose, trigger, owner, frequency, inputs, steps, and outputs plus a storage location and a quality check — so that any of them can be picked up cold, including by you six months from now with no memory of writing it.

Layered on top of the SOPs is a fixed admin calendar: weekly (receivables review, CRM/status updates, expense recording, deadline check, active project financial status), monthly (close revenue and expenses, export bank data, save subscription invoices, review tool costs and cash flow, prep accountant notes, review project profitability), quarterly (pricing review, revenue mix review, tax/accountant review, subscription and vendor contract review, emergency reserve review), and yearly (archive records, review the business model, review high-cost tools, review insurance/legal needs, set financial goals). The cadence matters more than the exact content — obligations calendarized in advance never become surprises.

## Key Concepts
- **SOP-worthy areas**: new client onboarding, invoice issuing, payment follow-up, expense recording, subscription review, monthly close, project financial close, accountant handoff, records backup, client offboarding, support request intake.
- **Required SOP fields**: purpose, trigger, owner, frequency, inputs, steps, outputs, storage location, quality check.
- **Admin calendar cadence**: weekly (receivables, CRM, expenses, deadlines), monthly (close, bank export, subscriptions, cash flow, accountant prep), quarterly (pricing, revenue mix, tax/vendor review, reserve review), yearly (archiving, business model review, tool audit, insurance/legal review, goal-setting).
- **Design principle**: keep each SOP simple enough to actually follow weekly — an SOP too elaborate to execute under normal workload pressure is worse than no SOP.
- **Forbidden patterns**: keeping recurring obligations only in memory, over-engineering SOPs into unusable complexity, skipping the monthly close because the business feels too small to need it, and leaving payment follow-up unscheduled.

## Example Code
A single SOP written in the standard format, ready to hand to future-you or a backup contact:

```md
## SOP: Monthly Close

**Purpose:** Turn the month's scattered invoices, payments, and expenses
  into a reviewed financial snapshot and a clean accountant handoff.
**Trigger:** Last business day of each month.
**Owner:** self
**Frequency:** Monthly
**Inputs:** invoice tracker, expense tracker, bank export, subscription
  register, active project list
**Steps:**
  1. Update every invoice's status (issued / paid / overdue).
  2. Reconcile payments received against the bank export.
  3. Categorize the month's expenses.
  4. Save all receipts and subscription invoices to the dated folder.
  5. Update the cash-flow forecast and reserve balances.
  6. Review profitability notes for each active project.
  7. Write one paragraph of accountant notes and one paragraph of
     lessons/next-month priorities.
**Outputs:** Monthly_Reviews/2026-07_close.md, updated dashboard snapshot
**Storage:** Business_Operations/Monthly_Reviews/
**Quality check:** every invoice has a status; every expense has a
  category; at least one next-month action is written down.
```
This single document is what turns "did I do the close this month?" from a memory question into a five-minute checklist that anyone — including a stressed future version of you — can execute correctly.

## When to Use
- When setting up business operations for the first time, before recurring obligations start slipping.
- Any time a recurring task has been missed or done late more than once — that's the signal it needs to become an SOP with a calendar trigger.
- When bringing in a trusted backup contact or assistant, so obligations don't depend solely on your memory.
- During the yearly review, to check whether existing SOPs still match how the business actually operates.

## Common Mistakes
- Keeping recurring financial and admin obligations only in memory instead of on a written, triggered calendar.
- Building SOPs so detailed and rigid they become too much friction to actually follow week after week.
- Skipping the monthly close on the reasoning that the business is "too small" to need this kind of discipline yet.
- Leaving payment follow-up as an ad hoc, unscheduled activity instead of a weekly calendar item.
