# 337. Selling Into a New Vertical Without a Case Study

## What It Is
Every strategic move into higher-value work or a new market segment starts with the same uncomfortable gap: the positioning you want doesn't yet have the proof to back it up. You want to say "I build production-ready platforms for logistics companies," but your actual portfolio is three unrelated SaaS MVPs and an admin panel for a bakery chain. Selling into that gap honestly — without fabricating experience — is a specific, learnable skill, not a matter of waiting until the case study magically appears.

The core move is a proof gap analysis: name the desired positioning, list existing proof honestly, identify exactly what's missing, and then find the fastest legitimate way to close that gap — often a smaller, lower-risk, possibly discounted first engagement in the new vertical, taken specifically to generate a real, referenceable case study, not for its revenue alone. This is different from lying about experience; it's deliberately trading margin or scope on one engagement for the proof asset the business needs to sell the next five at full price.

While the first vertical-specific proof is still being built, positioning can lean on adjacent, verifiable strengths instead of a fabricated niche claim: "I build custom web platforms, admin panels, and SaaS MVPs for businesses that need production-ready software instead of one-off pages" is honest and strong even with zero logistics-specific case studies. The transition should be gradual and never destroy current revenue — keep existing good clients, add the new positioning alongside the old, publish proof as it's created, and only reduce the low-fit legacy work once the new pipeline is actually validated.

## Key Concepts
- **Proof gap analysis**: explicitly write down the desired position, the existing proof, what's missing, the fastest legitimate proof asset to create, and what project would validate it.
- **First engagement as proof investment**: a smaller or discounted first project in a new vertical can be a deliberate trade of short-term margin for the case study needed to sell the next several at full value — as long as this is a conscious choice, not a default discount.
- **Adjacent honest positioning**: while vertical-specific proof doesn't exist yet, lean on genuinely true adjacent strengths (technical depth, process quality, related industry parallels) rather than an unsupported niche claim.
- **Messaging upgrade ladder**: move from generic ("I build websites and apps") to outcome-specific ("I build production-ready platforms for X") only as fast as real proof accumulates — never ahead of it.
- **Gradual transition rule**: keep current good clients and revenue while building the new positioning; don't abandon reliable work before the new pipeline is validated.
- **Claim verification discipline**: any public claim about experience or outcomes must be genuinely supportable — no invented case studies, no borrowed metrics, no implied client relationships that don't exist.
- **Sanitized proof as a bridge**: if an existing project can't be publicly named, a sanitized version (industry category, problem type, technical approach, anonymous testimonial) can still support positioning honestly.

## Example Code
A proof gap analysis used to plan entry into a new vertical:

```md
## Proof Gap Analysis

Desired position: "Technical partner for logistics/fleet SMEs building
  internal operations software."
Existing proof: 3 SaaS MVP case studies (unrelated verticals), 1 admin
  panel for a retail client (structurally similar: roles, CRUD, CSV
  import — but not logistics-specific).
Missing proof: any logistics-specific outcome, terminology fluency,
  domain-specific workflow understanding (dispatch, routing, compliance).
Fastest proof asset: a paid Workflow Automation Audit ($1,500, below
  normal rate) offered to one logistics contact via a warm referral,
  explicitly framed internally as a proof-building engagement.
Project needed to validate: deliver that audit well, convert the
  findings call into a small fixed-price fix project, document it as
  a full case study with permission.
Content needed: one LinkedIn post connecting the retail admin-panel
  experience to logistics operational needs, published now, ahead of
  the case study, using only currently true claims.

Interim honest pitch used until the case study lands:
  "I build role-based internal operations software — inventory, CRM-
  lite, and workflow tools — for SMEs replacing spreadsheet-based
  processes. I'm currently applying that specifically to logistics
  and fleet operations."
```
Nothing in the interim pitch is fabricated — it's the true adjacent experience, stated honestly, while the actual logistics proof is being deliberately built through one real engagement.

## When to Use
- When deliberately trying to move into a higher-value niche or vertical where you currently have thin or no direct proof.
- Before writing any positioning copy that claims expertise you can't yet fully back up — to check the claim against actual proof first.
- When deciding whether to take a smaller or discounted first project specifically because it would generate a needed proof asset.
- When a warm referral into a new vertical appears — to recognize it as a proof-gap-closing opportunity, not just one more project.

## Common Mistakes
- Claiming a niche or vertical expertise publicly with no genuine proof behind it, which collapses the first time a prospect asks a specific question.
- Waiting passively for the "right" case study to appear instead of deliberately engineering a first engagement to create it.
- Abandoning reliable current work and revenue before the new positioning's pipeline has actually been validated.
- Treating every discount in a new vertical as a proof investment, without consciously tracking whether it's actually producing a usable case study afterward.

## Further Reading
- *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* — Al Ries and Jack Trout: the classic framing of positioning as a claim that must be earned and defensible, not just declared.
- *Winning Without Pitching* — Blair Enns: on building authority and proof deliberately rather than accidentally.
