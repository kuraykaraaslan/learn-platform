## What It Is
Before selecting metrics, building a dashboard, or planning content, a solo software business needs to define what "growth" actually means for it — and growth is not audience size. For a one-person operation, growth means a repeatable path from visibility to qualified revenue, and that path deserves a single North Star metric that everything else supports. The recommended North Star for a solo software business is qualified project opportunities per month, where "qualified" has a specific, non-negotiable definition: a lead with a clear business problem, a relevant service fit, a budget signal, a timeline signal, and access to or influence over the decision-maker. A metric that doesn't meet all five criteria isn't a qualified opportunity yet, no matter how promising the conversation felt.

The growth model behind that North Star is a chain of stages, each with its own supporting metrics: audience reach (impressions, profile views, search clicks) leads to trust asset engagement (saves, comments, case-study views), which leads to lead capture (form submissions, DMs, inquiries), then qualification, then a discovery call, then a proposal, then closed revenue, and finally referral or retainer — which feeds back into reach. Metrics should be kept in three distinct levels so activity never gets confused with outcome: business metrics (revenue, qualified leads, close rate) are what actually matter; pipeline metrics (discovery calls, proposals, follow-ups) are the leading indicators; activity metrics (posts published, messages sent, referrals asked) are inputs that only matter if they move the levels above them.

Because a solo operator's capacity is genuinely limited, targets should be directional rather than rigid enterprise-style OKRs — a useful starting range is 4-8 qualified opportunities per month, 2-4 discovery calls, 1-3 proposals, and one closed project or retainer expansion. Followers, likes, post count, website visitors, and impressions are explicitly the wrong choice for a North Star: they are inputs or signals at best, never the business outcome itself, and optimizing for them directly tends to produce vanity growth that never converts into revenue.

## Key Concepts
- **North Star metric**: qualified project opportunities per month — chosen specifically because it sits closest to revenue while still being something a solo operator can directly influence week to week.
- **Qualified opportunity definition**: a lead must have a clear business problem, relevant service fit, a budget signal, a timeline signal, and decision-maker access or influence — all five, not just some.
- **Eight-stage growth model**: audience reach → trust asset engagement → lead capture → qualification → discovery call → proposal → closed revenue → referral/retainer (which feeds back into reach).
- **Three-level metric hierarchy**: business metrics (revenue, qualified leads, close rate) as outcomes; pipeline metrics (calls, proposals, follow-ups) as leading indicators; activity metrics (posts, messages, referral asks) as inputs — never let activity substitute for outcome in a review.
- **Directional solo targets**: roughly 4-8 qualified opportunities, 2-4 discovery calls, and 1-3 proposals per month, with one closed project or retainer expansion — adjusted for project size and current capacity, not treated as a rigid quota.
- **Forbidden North Stars**: followers, likes, post count, website visitors, and impressions — all inputs or signals, never the actual business outcome.

## Example Code
A filled growth model definition for a solo software business, used as the reference point for every later dashboard and review:

```md
## Growth Model

North Star: Qualified project opportunities per month (target: 5-7)

Primary ICP: Ops leads and founders at 10-50 person SMEs replacing
  manual, spreadsheet-based workflows with software.

Main channels: LinkedIn technical case-study posts, referrals from
  past clients, SEO-driven inbound to two service pages.

Trust assets: 4 published case studies, a demo video of the most
  recent dashboard build, and a one-page "how I scope a project" doc.

Lead capture mechanism: intake form on the site (budget range,
  timeline, and problem description required fields) plus direct
  LinkedIn DMs routed into the same tracker.

Qualification criteria: clear business problem stated, service fit
  confirmed against current offer catalog, budget range disclosed,
  start timeline within 3 months, contact is the decision-maker or
  has direct access to them.

Monthly review metric: qualified opportunities created this month,
  broken down by source, compared against the 5-7 target range.
```
Every dashboard, weekly review, and experiment built afterward is checked against this one definition — if a proposed change doesn't move qualified opportunities per month, it's an activity, not a growth lever.

## When to Use
- Before building any growth dashboard, so the metrics chosen actually connect to the business outcome rather than to whatever is easiest to measure.
- When evaluating whether a marketing or content effort is "working" — check it against qualified opportunities, not against likes or impressions.
- At the start of each quarter, to confirm the North Star and ICP definition still match the business's current direction.
- When a new channel or tactic is proposed, to immediately classify whether it targets reach, trust, lead capture, qualification, or a later stage in the model.

## Common Mistakes
- Choosing followers, impressions, or website traffic as the primary growth metric because they're easy to track and always trending upward.
- Counting an unqualified lead (no budget signal, no timeline, no decision-maker access) as equivalent to a qualified one in the monthly count.
- Confusing activity metrics (posts published, messages sent) with actual outcomes during a review, and declaring a channel successful based on activity alone.
- Setting rigid, enterprise-style OKR targets that don't account for a solo operator's actual weekly capacity, leading to burnout chasing an arbitrary number.
