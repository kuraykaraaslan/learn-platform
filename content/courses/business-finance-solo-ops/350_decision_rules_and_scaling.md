# 350. Deciding When to Scale, Pause, or Kill a Growth Tactic

## What It Is
> This lesson is general education, not financial or tax advice. Capacity and scaling thresholds here are directional — set your own limits based on actual delivery history, not aspiration.

Scaling a growth tactic should happen only when there is real evidence it creates qualified business outcomes — not because it feels like it's working, not because it's the newest idea, and not because stopping feels like giving up. Every active tactic should be classified into exactly one of six states: start, continue, iterate, scale, pause, or stop, each with clear trigger conditions. Continue applies when there's a positive but not-yet-sufficient signal, or when the tactic supports strategic positioning or builds trust even without immediate leads. Iterate applies when there's attention but low conversion, leads but low qualification, or confusion showing up in replies and calls — the tactic isn't wrong, the execution needs adjustment. Scale applies only when a tactic reliably creates qualified leads and sales conversations, can be repeated without damaging quality, and fits current delivery capacity. Pause applies when a tactic consumes time without useful signal or distracts from stronger channels. Stop applies when a tactic damages positioning, attracts bad-fit clients, creates ethical concerns, or has produced repeated poor results even after a genuine iteration attempt.

Before scaling anything, a capacity check is mandatory: delivery capacity, follow-up capacity, lead quality, fit with current positioning, availability of proof assets, and whether the sales process itself is ready to handle more volume. Scaling lead generation when the sales and delivery system underneath it can't absorb the increase doesn't produce growth — it produces missed follow-ups, rushed delivery, and damaged relationships at a larger scale than before. The investment ladder formalizes how scaling should actually happen in sequence: manual test, then repeat manually, then build a template or process, then add light automation, then consider a paid tool, then paid distribution, and only then outsourcing. Jumping straight to paid ads or automation before a tactic has been validated manually multiplies an unproven approach instead of a proven one.

## Key Concepts
- **Six-way classification**: start, continue, iterate, scale, pause, stop — every active tactic should carry one of these labels at all times, not sit in an ambiguous "still trying it" state indefinitely.
- **Continue triggers**: positive but insufficient signal yet; supports strategic positioning; builds trust even without immediate leads.
- **Iterate triggers**: attention with low conversion, leads with low qualification, or confusion appearing in replies and calls — fix the execution before judging the channel.
- **Scale triggers**: creates qualified leads, creates real sales conversations, can be repeated without quality loss, and fits current delivery capacity — all four required.
- **Pause vs. stop**: pause when a tactic wastes time without signal or distracts from a stronger channel (reversible); stop when it damages positioning, attracts bad-fit clients, creates ethical concerns, or has failed repeatedly after a real iteration attempt (not reversible without starting over).
- **Investment ladder**: manual test → repeat manually → template/process → light automation → paid tool → paid distribution → outsourcing — never skip ahead of manual validation.

## Example Code
A quarterly decision worksheet classifying three active growth tactics using the six-way framework:

```md
## Growth Tactic Decision Review — Q3 2026

### Tactic 1: LinkedIn case-study posts (running 3 months)
Signal: qualified DM rate rose from 1.2 to 2.1 per post (see
  Lesson 349 experiment GR-014).
Capacity check: current admin/BD time budget can support current
  posting frequency; no additional cost.
Classification: SCALE — increase frequency from 2x/week to 3x/week,
  reuse the same format across the next two case studies.

### Tactic 2: Cold outbound email to a purchased list
Signal: 400 emails sent, 3 replies, 0 qualified leads, 2 spam
  complaints.
Capacity check: N/A — signal alone disqualifies this.
Classification: STOP — low quality, and spam complaints create a
  reputation risk that outweighs any possible future signal.

### Tactic 3: Partner referral arrangement with a design agency
Signal: 1 qualified referral in 4 months, currently in active
  proposal stage; relationship is new (see Lesson 344).
Capacity check: no capacity cost — maintenance is 1 call/month.
Classification: CONTINUE — one data point isn't enough to scale,
  but it supports positioning and costs almost nothing to maintain.
  Revisit at 6 months with a second data point.
```
Each tactic got a different verdict from the same six-way framework — the framework's job is to prevent all three from being judged the same way just because they're all "growth activities."

## When to Use
- After any experiment concludes, to translate the result into one of the six classifications rather than leaving it as an informal impression.
- During the monthly or quarterly growth review, to re-classify every currently active tactic and catch ones that have quietly drifted into "pause" territory without anyone deciding that.
- Before scaling anything, as a mandatory capacity check — confirming delivery, follow-up, and sales-process readiness, not just lead volume.
- When a tactic that once worked starts underperforming — to decide deliberately between iterate, pause, and stop rather than continuing on inertia.

## Common Mistakes
- Scaling a tactic based on impressions or engagement alone, without confirming it produces qualified leads and closed revenue.
- Increasing lead generation activity while follow-up is already falling behind, compounding a capacity problem instead of fixing it.
- Jumping straight to a paid tool or paid distribution before a tactic has been validated manually at small scale.
- Continuing a tactic indefinitely out of sunk-cost attachment, well past the point where repeated iteration attempts have failed to improve it.

## Further Reading
- *Scaling Up* — Verne Harnish: a broader operating framework for deciding what to grow and what to leave alone, adaptable down to a one-person business.
- *Good Strategy/Bad Strategy* — Richard Rumelt: on the discipline of choosing where to concentrate effort instead of pursuing every plausible growth idea at once.
