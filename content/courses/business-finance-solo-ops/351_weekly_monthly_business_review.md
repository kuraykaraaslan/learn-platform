# 351. Running a Weekly and Monthly Business Review

## What It Is
A weekly review and a monthly review answer different questions, and conflating them is why many solo operators either review too often to see real patterns or too rarely to catch problems while they're small. The weekly review is lightweight and tactical: what happened, what created signal, what needs follow-up, and what should change next week. It tracks new leads, qualified leads, discovery calls booked, proposals sent, deals won or lost, follow-ups sent and overdue, content published, and experiment status — and it deliberately limits next-week actions to three to five items, because a solo operator genuinely cannot execute twenty growth actions while also delivering client work.

The monthly review looks for patterns invisible at weekly resolution: channel quality, qualified lead sources, proposal conversion rate, content themes that actually built trust, offer clarity, and the reasons behind lost deals. It should conclude with exactly one primary growth theme for the coming month — increase qualified inbound, improve offer clarity, increase referrals, improve follow-up discipline — plus two or three supporting actions, not a long list of parallel initiatives that dilute focus. Both reviews should track metrics across five categories: revenue, pipeline, retention, proof, and capacity — and specific metric combinations carry diagnostic meaning on their own: revenue up with margin down usually points to underpricing or scope creep; leads up with close rate down usually points to a wrong ICP or weak qualification; close rate high with capacity full is actually a signal to raise prices or productize, not a problem to solve by working more hours; and low recurring revenue is a signal to offer maintenance, reactivate old clients, or create an advisory retainer.

The review cadence itself nests cleanly: weekly covers pipeline and follow-up; monthly covers revenue, source quality, and offer performance; quarterly (built from the same monthly data, aggregated) covers positioning, pricing, service mix, client quality, and capacity bottlenecks. Skipping the weekly layer and only doing monthly reviews means follow-ups go stale for weeks at a time; skipping the monthly layer and only doing weekly reviews means slow-moving problems like margin erosion or client concentration never surface until they're already serious.

## Key Concepts
- **Weekly review scope**: what happened, what created signal, what needs follow-up, what changes next week — tracked via new/qualified leads, calls booked, proposals sent, deals won/lost, follow-ups (sent and overdue), content published, and experiment status.
- **Monthly review scope**: channel quality, qualified lead sources, proposal conversion, content themes, offer clarity, and lost-deal reasons — concluding in exactly one primary focus theme plus two to three supporting actions.
- **Five metric categories**: revenue, pipeline, retention, proof, capacity — tracked at both weekly and monthly resolution so busyness is never mistaken for progress.
- **Metric interpretation patterns**: revenue up/margin down → underpricing or scope creep; leads up/close rate down → wrong ICP or weak qualification; close rate high/capacity full → raise prices or productize; recurring revenue low → offer maintenance, reactivate past clients, or build a retainer.
- **Action limit rule**: weekly reviews cap next-week actions at three to five; monthly reviews cap next-month focus at one primary theme plus two to three supporting actions.
- **Nested cadence**: weekly → pipeline/follow-up; monthly → revenue/source quality/offer performance; quarterly → positioning/pricing/service mix/client quality/capacity.

## Example Code
A filled weekly review followed by the monthly review it feeds into:

```md
# Weekly Growth Review — Week of Aug 17

## Pipeline
New leads: 4        Qualified leads: 2      Discovery calls: 2
Proposals: 1         Won/lost: 0 / 1 (lost - budget mismatch)
Overdue follow-ups: 1 (Client C proposal, sent 9 days ago)

## Channel Signals
Best source: LinkedIn case-study post (2 of 4 leads)
Worst source: cold outbound (0 replies from 20 sent)
Best content asset: reporting-dashboard case study
Best referral/partner signal: none this week

## Experiment Review
Running experiment: GR-014 (case-study post format)
Current signal: 2.1 qualified DMs/post avg, above 1.2 baseline
Decision: continue through planned duration

## Lessons
What worked: case-study format outperforming technical recaps.
What did not work: cold outbound list, zero qualified signal.
What surprised me: lost deal was budget mismatch, not fit.

## Next Week Actions
1. Follow up on Client C proposal (overdue).
2. Pause cold outbound, reallocate time to 1 more case-study post.
3. Ask lost-deal contact one feedback question (budget range gap).

---

# Monthly Growth Review — August 2026

Total leads: 19        Qualified rate: 42%     Discovery rate: 75%
Proposal rate: 60%      Close rate: 33%         Revenue won: EUR 9,000
Best channel by qualified lead: LinkedIn (8 of 8 qualified leads)
Top lost-deal reason: budget mismatch (3 of 5 losses this month)

Next month focus: Improve offer clarity — add a visible price band
  to the service page so budget-mismatched leads self-filter before
  a proposal is written.
Supporting actions:
  1. Add price-range field to intake form.
  2. Retire cold outbound entirely; reallocate to referral asks.
```
The weekly overdue-follow-up flag and the monthly lost-deal pattern pointed to the same root cause — budget mismatch — which became the single focus for the next month instead of five scattered fixes.

## When to Use
- Every week, on a fixed day, as a short standing ritual — not skipped during busy delivery weeks, since that's exactly when follow-ups go stale.
- At the end of each month, to step back from weekly noise and look for patterns in channel quality, close rate, and lost-deal reasons.
- Whenever a metric combination shows up that has a known diagnostic meaning (e.g., revenue up but margin down) — treat it as a prompt to investigate immediately rather than waiting for the next scheduled review.
- When deciding what to focus on next month — use the monthly review's single-theme rule instead of running several initiatives in parallel.

## Common Mistakes
- Skipping the weekly review during busy weeks, which is exactly when overdue follow-ups and stalled proposals are most likely to go unnoticed.
- Setting more than one primary focus for the coming month, which dilutes the limited hours available to a solo operator across too many initiatives.
- Judging performance by activity metrics (posts published, messages sent) instead of the outcome metrics they're supposed to support.
- Noticing a diagnostic pattern (e.g., leads up, close rate down) but not tracing it to a specific cause before the next review cycle.
