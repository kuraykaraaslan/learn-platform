# 352. Ethical Growth: What Not to Do

## What It Is
> This lesson is general education, not financial or tax advice. Data collection and consent requirements vary by jurisdiction — confirm specific privacy obligations with a qualified advisor if handling any sensitive personal data.

Growth tactics for a freelance or solo software business should increase trust, not manipulate people — and this isn't just a values statement, it's a practical constraint, because a reputation-dependent business that gets caught using deceptive growth tactics loses far more than any single tactic could have gained. The standard every growth tactic must meet is: truthful, permission-aware, privacy-conscious, non-deceptive, respectful of boundaries, and aligned with real capabilities. Clear positioning, useful technical content, honest case studies, permission-based testimonials, respectful outbound, transparent calls-to-action, reasonable follow-up, and privacy-conscious analytics are all allowed and encouraged — none of them require manipulation to work.

A specific list of tactics is simply off-limits regardless of how effective they might seem: fake testimonials, fake scarcity, fake client logos, fake metrics, scraped personal data abuse, spam automation, misleading guarantees, bait-and-switch pricing, dark-pattern lead capture, and using confidential client data as proof without permission. Any public claim also needs to survive a verification test — "I guarantee your SaaS will succeed" is an unsupportable promise that invites exactly the kind of scrutiny that destroys credibility, while "I help reduce delivery risk by clarifying scope, defining MVP workflows, and documenting handover" is specific, honest, and defensible under questioning. The same discipline applies to data: collect only what's needed for qualification and follow-up, and never store sensitive personal data without a clear project reason and appropriate consent — and to outreach: personalized and relevant is fine, aggressive sequences, misleading subject lines, and pressure-based messaging are not.

The simplest working test for whether a growth tactic is acceptable is whether you would still be comfortable if the prospect, the client, or the public saw exactly how it works. Fake scarcity ("only 2 spots left!" when that isn't true) fails this test instantly, because seeing the mechanism destroys the trust the tactic was trying to build. An honest capacity statement ("I'm currently booking projects starting in October — happy to hold a discovery call sooner") produces a similar sense of urgency without any deception behind it, and it survives being seen for exactly what it is.

## Key Concepts
- **The standard**: every growth tactic must be truthful, permission-aware, privacy-conscious, non-deceptive, respectful of boundaries, and aligned with real capabilities.
- **Allowed patterns**: clear positioning, useful technical content, honest case studies, permission-based testimonials, respectful outbound, transparent CTAs, reasonable follow-up, privacy-conscious analytics.
- **Forbidden patterns**: fake testimonials, fake scarcity, fake client logos, fake metrics, scraped personal data abuse, spam automation, misleading guarantees, bait-and-switch pricing, dark-pattern lead capture, confidential client data used as proof without permission.
- **Claim verification rule**: any public claim must be supportable under direct questioning — replace unverifiable guarantees with specific, honest descriptions of process and approach.
- **Privacy rule**: collect only what's needed for qualification and follow-up; never store sensitive personal data without a clear project reason and explicit consent.
- **Done-criteria test**: a tactic is acceptable when you'd still be comfortable if the prospect, client, or public saw exactly how it works.

## Example Code
Three before/after rewrites applying the claim verification rule and the done-criteria test to common growth tactics:

```md
## Rewrite 1 — Fake Scarcity

Before: "Only 2 spots left this month! Book now before they're gone."
  (Not true — no actual capacity limit exists.)
After:  "I'm currently booking new projects starting in October.
  Happy to schedule a discovery call sooner if you want to plan
  ahead of that."
Why it passes: states a real constraint (booking timeline), creates
  legitimate urgency, survives being seen exactly as it is.

## Rewrite 2 — Unverifiable Guarantee

Before: "I guarantee your MVP will succeed in the market."
After:  "I help reduce delivery risk by clarifying scope early,
  defining a realistic MVP feature set, and documenting the
  handover so the product is maintainable after launch."
Why it passes: describes real, controllable actions instead of an
  outcome no developer can actually guarantee.

## Rewrite 3 — Confidential Data as Proof

Before: Publishing a client's internal dashboard screenshot with
  real revenue figures visible, without asking first.
After:  A sanitized case study describing the problem, approach,
  and directional result ("reduced weekly reporting time by over
  80%") with client permission and no real figures or screenshots
  of internal systems.
Why it passes: preserves the proof value of the story without
  exposing data the client never agreed to make public.
```
Each rewrite keeps the persuasive intent of the original tactic while removing the part that would collapse trust if the mechanism were seen clearly.

## When to Use
- Before publishing any growth-related claim, testimonial, or urgency statement — run it through the claim verification and done-criteria tests first.
- When designing a lead capture form or landing page, to check for dark patterns (forced continuity, hidden opt-outs, confusing defaults) before it goes live.
- Whenever a growth tactic feels unusually effective very quickly — a useful moment to double-check it isn't effective because it's manipulative.
- Before using any client-related data, screenshot, or metric as proof, to confirm permission was actually given for that specific use.

## Common Mistakes
- **The landing page says "spots filling fast" with no actual capacity limit behind it** — Using soft scarcity or urgency language ("spots filling fast") that isn't actually true, treating it as harmless marketing convention rather than a fabricated claim.
- **The client's testimonial gets tightened up with a stronger line that wasn't quite what they said** — Publishing a testimonial that was lightly edited or invented to sound stronger than what the client actually said.
- **The intake form asks for company revenue and team size details that have nothing to do with qualifying the lead** — Collecting more personal data on an intake form than is actually needed for qualification, without a clear reason or stated purpose.
- **Every competitor uses countdown timers on their pricing page, so one gets added here too** — Assuming a tactic is fine because "everyone does it" rather than checking it against the specific truthful/permission-aware/privacy-conscious standard.

## Further Reading

- *This Is Marketing* — Seth Godin: a case for permission-based, trust-building marketing as a durable strategy rather than a compliance burden.
