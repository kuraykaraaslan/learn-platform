## What It Is
In a solo business, burnout isn't just a personal wellbeing issue — it's a business continuity risk with the same structural shape as a stolen laptop or a long illness, because the business has a bus factor of exactly one. If the one person operating it stops functioning, every client SLA, every active project, and every piece of pipeline is simultaneously at risk, which is why burnout deserves the same proactive detection and response discipline as any other single-point-of-failure scenario. The first defense is recognizing which work is energy-positive and which is energy-negative before accepting it: technically interesting projects with clear scope, reliable payment, and direct access to a decision-maker tend to sustain output over time, while undefined or constantly shifting scope, indecisive clients, and irregular payment behavior drain capacity faster than the invoice value can justify. Weighing energy impact alongside financial return when evaluating new work is not a luxury — a project that pays well but drains energy costs more than the invoice shows, in the form of degraded quality on every other commitment running alongside it.

Early burnout signals are specific and should never be dismissed as ordinary tiredness: difficulty starting to code, irregular sleep, small tasks starting to feel disproportionately large, irritation at ordinary client messages, chronic procrastination, and spikes of perfectionism driven by fear rather than confidence. When three or more of these signals show up in the same week, a mandatory load review is triggered — not optional, not something to push past because a deadline is close. The response protocol that follows is concrete: identify which commitments are generating the most pressure, communicate proactively with affected clients about adjusted timelines, cancel or defer non-critical meetings for the next several days, protect several evenings as genuine recovery time rather than catch-up time, decline anything new until load normalizes, and log what triggered the episode so intake decisions can be adjusted going forward.

A weekly self-assessment keeps this from being reactive only: rating energy level, output quality, client relationship health, pipeline confidence, and personal satisfaction on a simple scale, with a firm rule that if two or more categories score low, the following week's intake is restricted to existing commitments only — no new client conversations, no new proposals, until the numbers recover. Underneath the weekly check sits a physical baseline that isn't optional during high-load periods: a sleep minimum, a minimum weekly exercise target, and a hard rule that impaired sleep defers any critical decision, because a bad pricing or contract decision made on four hours of sleep costs far more than the one-day delay would have.

## Key Concepts
- **Burnout as a continuity risk**: because a solo business has a bus factor of one, personal burnout threatens every active client commitment simultaneously — it deserves the same proactive planning as illness or equipment failure.
- **Energy-positive vs. energy-negative work**: energy-positive work has clear scope, technical interest, reliable payment, and decision-maker access; energy-negative work has shifting scope, indecisive clients, irregular payment, and constant interruption — weigh energy cost alongside revenue when evaluating new work.
- **Six early burnout signals**: difficulty starting to code, sleep irregularity, small tasks feeling large, irritation at client messages, chronic procrastination, perfectionism spikes. Three or more in the same week triggers a mandatory load review.
- **Burnout response protocol**: identify the highest-pressure commitments, communicate proactively with affected clients, defer non-critical meetings, protect several recovery evenings, decline new intake until load normalizes, and log the trigger for future intake adjustments.
- **Weekly capacity self-assessment**: rate energy, output quality, client relationship health, pipeline confidence, and personal satisfaction 1-5; two or more scores at 2 or below restricts next week's intake to existing commitments only.
- **Physical baseline as a business input**: a firm sleep minimum, regular exercise even during high-load periods, and a rule that impaired sleep defers critical decisions rather than being treated as an acceptable tradeoff.

## Example Code
A weekly capacity self-assessment that triggers the response protocol, logged the way a continuity incident would be:

```md
## Weekly Capacity Self-Assessment — Week of Aug 24

Energy level:            2 / 5
Output quality:          2 / 5
Client relationship health: 3 / 5
Pipeline confidence:     4 / 5
Personal satisfaction:   2 / 5

Three categories at or below 2 -> MANDATORY LOAD REVIEW TRIGGERED.

Signals observed this week (from the early-signal list):
  - Difficulty starting to code most mornings (4 of 5 days).
  - Irritated by a routine client status-check message.
  - Sleep averaging ~5.5 hours, below the 7-hour minimum.
  -> 3 signals present in the same week, confirming the trigger.

Response protocol executed:
  1. Highest-pressure commitment identified: Client A's fixed
     deadline, self-imposed tighter than the contract requires.
  2. Client A contacted proactively: deadline moved 4 days, framed
     honestly as a capacity adjustment, not hidden.
  3. Two non-critical meetings this week cancelled and rescheduled.
  4. Next 4 evenings blocked as recovery time — no client work.
  5. Declined one new inbound lead this week; queued a response
     for after the load review period ends.
  6. Logged trigger: two overlapping deadlines self-imposed in the
     same week without checking actual contract dates first.

Next week's intake restriction: existing commitments only, per the
  self-assessment rule (2+ categories at or below 2).
```
The load review didn't wait for output to visibly collapse — it triggered on the leading signals, the same way an SLA breach protocol triggers on a shrinking time window rather than after the deadline has already passed.

## When to Use
- Every week, as a standing five-question self-assessment — not only when something already feels wrong.
- The moment three or more early burnout signals appear within the same week, as an unconditional trigger for a load review, regardless of how close any deadline is.
- Before accepting a new project or client, to weigh its energy cost (scope clarity, client behavior, payment reliability) alongside its revenue, not revenue alone.
- Before any critical decision, as a check on whether sleep and stress levels are adequate enough to trust the judgment behind it.

## Common Mistakes
- Dismissing early burnout signals as ordinary tiredness or a rough week, and only responding once output has visibly and significantly declined.
- Accepting a high-paying but energy-negative project without weighting its likely drag on the quality of every other concurrent commitment.
- Pushing through a triggered load review because a deadline feels close, rather than communicating proactively with the client and adjusting the timeline.
- Making a pricing, contract, or scope decision on inadequate sleep because the client is waiting, instead of deferring with a specific follow-up time.

## Further Reading
- *Burnout: The Secret to Unlocking the Stress Cycle* — Emily Nagoski and Amelia Nagoski: on the physiological difference between the stressor and the stress itself, and why removing the task doesn't automatically resolve the stress response.
- *The Power of Full Engagement* — Jim Loehr and Tony Schwartz: a framework for managing energy, not just time, as the real constraint on sustained output.
- This lesson is general education, not financial, tax, or medical advice. Persistent burnout signals that don't resolve with rest and load reduction warrant a conversation with a qualified healthcare provider.
