# Capstone — The Best Quarter You Cannot Pay For

## Brief
A solo operator sends you their Q3 figures and says it was their best quarter
yet. They want to know whether they can afford to take two weeks off in
November.

Here is everything they have.

**The quarter**
- Invoiced: **£72,000** (Q2 was £58,000)
- Cash actually received in the quarter: **£41,000**
- Outstanding: **£31,000**, of which **£18,000** is more than 30 days old
- Bank balance today: **£9,400** (start of the quarter: £16,200)
- A tax settlement of **£11,000** falls due next month. Nothing is set aside;
  it has been on the calendar since March.

**The clients**
- Northwind: **£43,200** invoiced this quarter
- Four other clients share the remaining £28,800
- The pipeline has the same two prospects it had in Q2, both unquoted

**The big project**
- "Atlas portal", fixed price **£18,000**, delivered in August
- Tracked time: **210 hours**, all of it logged while writing code
- Calls, revisions and the two "quick fixes" after launch were not tracked;
  the operator's own guess is "about another 60 hours"
- A quote for a similar portal went out in September at **£18,500**, because
  Atlas "went fine"

**The overheads**
- Software subscriptions: **£640/month**, up from £410 — three renewals
  landed in the quarter and none was reviewed

## Deliverable
A written diagnosis. Not advice about what to do with their life — findings
about this business, in the order you would raise them.

It is done when it contains:

1. **The answer to the question they asked**, stated first and plainly. They
   asked whether they can take two weeks off in November; say yes or no and
   say what it depends on.
2. **Findings, ranked by what happens if nothing changes.** Each one needs the
   number that shows it, not an impression. "Client concentration is high" is
   not a finding; the concentration ratio and what it would cost if that client
   left is.
3. **The one number they should compute before anything else**, and how. You
   are allowed exactly one, so choosing it is part of the exercise.
4. **What you could not tell from these figures.** There is a great deal, and
   a diagnosis that does not say so invites them to act on it as if it were
   complete.

Write it as if they will read it once, on a phone, and act on the first two
paragraphs.

## Reference Walkthrough
One competent diagnosis. Read it after scoring yourself.

**The answer, first.** No — not on these figures. Two weeks off in November is
a cash question, not a workload question, and the cash does not currently
cover next month's obligation, let alone a fortnight of nothing being invoiced.
It becomes a yes if £18,000 of the aged receivables lands before the tax
settlement, and that is a collections question they can start today.

**Finding 1 — the business is illiquid, not unprofitable.** £72,000 invoiced,
£41,000 received, £9,400 in the bank against £11,000 due next month. Nothing
here says the work is unprofitable; it says the money arrives later than the
obligations do. That distinction decides what to fix: chasing £18,000 of aged
invoices changes the position this month, and repricing does not.

**Finding 2 — the tax settlement was never a surprise.** It has been dated
since March, and no reserve exists. This is the finding to raise second, not
because it is larger than the concentration risk, but because it is the one
with a deadline. A standing transfer of a fixed percentage of every payment
received, into an account they do not spend from, removes this class of event
permanently.

**Finding 3 — Atlas's real rate is unknown, and September was priced from the
unknown.** £18,000 over the 210 logged hours is £85.71 an hour. The operator's
own estimate of unlogged calls, revisions and quick fixes puts it near 270
hours, which is £66.67. That is a 22% difference between the rate they believe
they charge and the rate they may actually be earning, and the September quote
of £18,500 was set from the first number. The correction is not "charge more";
it is to establish what the last project cost before pricing the next one.

**Finding 4 — Northwind is 60% of the quarter and the pipeline has not moved.**
Concentration on its own is a fact about a good client. Concentration plus a
pipeline containing the same two unquoted prospects as last quarter is an
exposure with no exit: if Northwind pauses, revenue does not fall by 60% — it
falls by 60% with nothing scheduled to replace it.

**Finding 5 — the overheads moved 56% and nobody decided it.** £410 to £640 a
month is £2,760 a year, arriving through three renewals none of which was a
decision. It is the smallest number here and the easiest to act on, which is
exactly why it should be raised last rather than first.

**The one number to compute first.** The real effective hourly rate across the
quarter: every hour actually worked, tracked or not, divided into cash actually
received. Not invoiced — received. It is the number that tells them whether the
pricing problem and the cash problem are the same problem, and every other
decision here depends on which one it is.

**What these figures cannot tell you.** Whether the £18,000 of aged receivables
is collectable or disputed. Whether Northwind's £43,200 is one contract or
several. What the operator's own drawings are, which is the difference between
a business that is tight and a person who is not being paid. Whether the 60
unlogged hours are an estimate or a guess. And nothing at all about November's
committed work, which is the actual input to the question they asked.

A diagnosis that does not list these implies it saw them.
