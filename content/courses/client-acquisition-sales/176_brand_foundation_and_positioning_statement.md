## What It Is
Before you write a single outreach message, LinkedIn headline, or proposal, you need an answer to a boring but load-bearing question: what do you actually say when someone asks "what do you do?" Most engineers answer with a job title and a tech stack — "full-stack developer, React and Node" — which is true and useless. It gives the listener nothing to remember you by and nothing to refer you for. A brand foundation is the set of decisions that make that answer specific: who you serve, what problem you solve for them, what category you compete in, and what you refuse to be known for.

A niche is the mechanism that makes positioning concrete. It can be built from an audience (SMEs with manual workflows), a problem (SaaS MVP launch), a solution type (admin panels and backoffice systems), a delivery model (solo technical partner vs. white-label agency support), or some combination of these — a compound niche. The strongest niches combine at least two of these axes, because "audience" alone ("I work with startups") or "technology" alone ("I'm a Next.js developer") is too thin to filter anything. A market category is the shelf you put yourself on next to the alternatives a client is already considering — cheap freelancer, agency, internal hire, no-code tool, or doing nothing. Naming the category tells the client which comparison to run, and running the comparison you want is half of winning it.

The positioning statement is where all of this compresses into one or two sentences you can actually reuse: in a LinkedIn headline, a cold email's second sentence, a proposal's opening line, a website hero. The standard formula is `I help <specific audience> achieve <business outcome> by <solution category>, with <differentiator/proof>`. Notice what's missing from that formula: your tech stack. Technology supports the promise; it should almost never lead it, because a non-technical buyer can't evaluate "PostgreSQL and Redis" but can evaluate "replace your spreadsheet-based order tracking with a system your team can trust."

None of this is permanent. A brand foundation is a working hypothesis you commit to long enough to test it against real leads, not a personality you adopt forever. But it has to exist in written form before acquisition work starts, because every later asset — the ICP, the offer packaging, the cold email, the landing page — inherits its language from here. Skipping this step doesn't save time; it just means you reinvent your positioning inconsistently in every asset you write.

## Key Concepts
- **Brand role**: The category label a client would use to describe you to someone else — "solo technical partner," not "guy who codes."
- **Compound niche**: A niche defined by two or more of audience, problem, solution, delivery model, or technology, rather than one axis alone.
- **Market category**: The shelf of alternatives (cheap freelancer, agency, internal hire, no-code tool, doing nothing) your positioning implicitly compares you against.
- **Positioning statement formula**: `I help <audience> achieve <outcome> by <solution>, with <differentiator>` — audience and outcome lead; stack trails.
- **Operating beliefs**: The stated convictions ("MVP does not mean low quality") that make your boundaries legible instead of arbitrary.
- **Boundaries**: What you explicitly refuse to be known for — cheapest option, unlimited revisions, generalist "I can build anything."
- **Message hierarchy**: Who I help → their problem → the outcome I create → how I deliver it → why I'm credible → what happens next — the order every asset should follow.
- **Repositioning**: Treat position as revisable based on evidence (leads, close rates, referral language), not as an identity to defend.

## When to Use
- Before writing any outreach message, bio, or landing page — it's the input every later asset depends on.
- When you notice you're being compared only on price, which usually means your positioning is too generic to filter.
- When inbound inquiries are consistently outside your expertise — a sign the positioning isn't doing its filtering job.
- When rewriting a LinkedIn headline, proposal intro, or website hero — always derive it from the foundation, don't freestyle it each time.
- Quarterly, or after a run of leads that don't match who you meant to attract.

## Common Mistakes
- Leading with a technology list instead of an audience and outcome — it forces the reader to do the translation work you should be doing for them.
- Positioning broadly "to not miss any leads," which produces the generalist trap: every prospect treats you as interchangeable with every other developer.
- Writing a positioning statement once and never testing it against real conversations — it should change when evidence says it's wrong, not never and not every week.
- Choosing a niche that sounds impressive but has no proof behind it yet — positioning without proof is just a claim.
