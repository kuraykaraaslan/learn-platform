## What It Is
Every claim you make in your positioning — "production-ready," "secure," "business-oriented" — is worthless until it's attached to evidence a skeptical stranger can check. Proof and credibility work as a matrix, not a pile: for each claim, there's a specific artifact type that backs it up, and a specific place it belongs. "Production-ready" is backed by deployment notes, a security/testing checklist, and handover documentation — not by the word "production-ready" appearing more emphatically. "Business-oriented" is backed by a workflow diagnosis or an MVP-scoping story, not by saying "I think about business impact." The discipline is to write the claim, then immediately ask what artifact would make a stranger believe it, and if no answer exists, either build the artifact or stop making the claim.

Proof comes in five recognizable categories: project proof (screenshots, demos, case studies), process proof (your discovery checklist, your handover checklist, your milestone structure), technical proof (architecture notes, schema explanations, a public repo), social proof (testimonials, referrals, client quotes), and authority proof (articles, talks, open-source work). A minimum viable proof stack for a solo operator starting out is smaller than it sounds: three real case studies, one process page, one handover checklist, one technical architecture writeup, and three testimonials. You don't need all five categories saturated before you start selling — you need enough that every major claim in your positioning has at least one thing behind it.

Founder narrative is a different kind of proof — not "here's evidence I did the work" but "here's why you can trust how I think." It matters because a solo freelancer is a bet on a person, not a company with redundancy, and buyers are implicitly asking why you work the way you do. The useful structure is background → realization → point of view → what you build → how you work → who you help. The realization is the load-bearing beat: not "I've always loved coding" (irrelevant) but "I noticed most software projects fail on unclear scope and handover, not on code quality" (a stance that explains your entire process). A founder narrative that skips the realization and jumps straight to a list of skills reads like a resume in paragraph form, which defeats the purpose — a resume proves capability, not judgment, and judgment is what a buyer is actually purchasing from a solo consultant.

Both proof and narrative fail the same way when done badly: they become generic. "Passionate about clean code" and "I deliver secure, scalable solutions" are unfalsifiable, and a buyer's nervous system has learned to discount unfalsifiable claims automatically. The fix is always the same — get specific enough that the sentence would be false if said about someone else.

## Key Concepts
- **Claim-to-proof matrix**: Every positioning claim needs a mapped artifact type — no artifact, no claim.
- **Five proof categories**: Project, process, technical, social, authority — a healthy proof stack draws from more than one.
- **Minimum viable proof stack**: Three case studies, one process page, one handover checklist, one architecture writeup, three testimonials — a realistic starting target.
- **Sanitized case study**: A way to show real work under an NDA — describe the situation and your role without naming the client or exposing private data.
- **Founder narrative structure**: Background → realization → point of view → what you build → how you work → who you help.
- **The realization beat**: The specific insight that explains why you work the way you do — the part that makes the narrative a stance, not a CV.
- **Falsifiability test**: A claim or story detail is credible only if it would be false when applied to someone else — genericness is the tell of an unproven claim.

**Claim-to-proof audit** (run this on your own bio, website, or LinkedIn About):

```markdown
| Claim I make               | Proof artifact that backs it up      | Where it lives         |
|-----------------------------|---------------------------------------|-------------------------|
| "Production-ready delivery" | Deployment + testing checklist        | Portfolio, proposal     |
| "Business-oriented"         | MVP scoping case study                | Case study page         |
| "Secure by default"         | Auth/permissions architecture note    | Technical writeup       |
| (add your own claim)        | (leave blank if no artifact exists)   | (this row needs work)   |
```

**Founder narrative skeleton** (fill and trim to 4-6 sentences for a bio; expand for an About page):

```text
Background:    I started from <relevant technical/business background>.
Realization:   Over time I noticed <the specific pattern about why projects
               succeed or fail that most people miss>.
Point of view: I believe <a stance that follows from the realization>.
What I build:  Today I work on <service categories>.
How I work:    My process is <the specific discipline this belief produces>.
Who I help:    ...for <specific audience>.
```

## When to Use
- Before publishing any bio, About page, or founder story — write the realization sentence first, everything else follows from it.
- When auditing existing marketing copy for claims that have no backing evidence.
- When deciding what to build next as a portfolio asset — build toward the claim currently missing proof, not toward whatever's easiest.
- When a prospect asks "how do I know you can deliver?" — you should be able to answer with an artifact, not a reassurance.
- When client confidentiality prevents naming a project — use the sanitized case-study format instead of dropping the proof entirely.

## Common Mistakes
- Making a claim ("secure," "production-ready," "business-oriented") with no specific artifact behind it — this is the single most common credibility failure.
- Writing a founder story that's really a resume in paragraph form, listing skills and jobs without ever stating the realization that explains your point of view.
- Oversharing unrelated personal history in a founder narrative instead of keeping it tied to professional judgment and relevance.
- Waiting until every project is "impressive enough" to publish as proof, instead of starting with three honest, well-written case studies now.
