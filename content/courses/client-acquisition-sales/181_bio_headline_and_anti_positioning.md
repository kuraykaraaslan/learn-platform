# 181. Bio, Headline, Anti-Positioning & Review Cadence

## What It Is
Your bio, headline, and profile are where positioning meets the ten-second attention span of someone scrolling LinkedIn or landing on your site. A profile has to do six jobs almost simultaneously: state your market role, name your target client, name the outcome, list service categories, signal proof or process, and give a next step. Most developer profiles do zero of these — they read like a CV summary ("Full-stack developer with 5 years of experience in React, Node.js...") which answers a question nobody asked ("what have you done") instead of the question a buyer actually has ("can you solve my specific problem, and what do I do next"). A working LinkedIn headline formula looks like `<market role> for <audience> | <core service categories> | <proof or process signal>` — for example, "Solo Technical Partner for SaaS MVPs & Admin Panels | React, Next.js, Node.js, PostgreSQL | From Scope to Deployment." Visual identity (consistent fonts, screenshot framing, a repeatable post template) supports this but is secondary — it makes existing proof look organized, it doesn't create proof on its own.

Anti-positioning is the underused half of brand strategy: deciding, in writing, what you refuse to be known for. Most freelancers only think about what to say yes to; the boundaries — "I am not positioned as the cheapest developer," "I don't build vague platform clones without discovery," "I don't offer unlimited revisions" — do real filtering work before a lead ever reaches a call. A bad-fit client self-selects out when your public copy already signals that you require written scope and milestone payment; without that signal, you find out the hard way, mid-project. The trick is tone: a boundary stated as a professional standard ("best fit for clients who value clear scope over the cheapest possible implementation") repels the wrong client; the same boundary stated as an attitude ("don't waste my time") repels good clients too.

None of this should be treated as permanent once written. Positioning review cadence — a light monthly pass and a deeper quarterly one — is what keeps your bio and boundaries honest against actual lead quality instead of vibes. Monthly, you ask which leads came in and whether they matched the audience you intended, which content created real conversations, and which CTA or headline might need a small edit. Quarterly, you ask harder questions: is the niche still commercially attractive, are you still being compared mainly on price, should the primary audience change. The rule that keeps this from becoming chaos is: small edits (headline wording, CTA phrasing) can happen often; core positioning changes only when repeated evidence — not one bad call — shows a real pattern.

## Key Concepts
- **Profile's six jobs**: Market role, target client, outcome, service categories, proof signal, CTA — a strong bio hits all six in a few sentences.
- **Headline formula**: `<market role> for <audience> | <service categories> | <proof/process signal>`.
- **CV-summary failure mode**: A bio that lists experience and skills instead of naming who it's for and what happens next.
- **Anti-positioning**: An explicit written list of what you refuse to be known for — a filter, not just a value proposition's negative space.
- **Boundary tone**: Framing a boundary as a professional standard, not an attitude, so it repels bad-fit leads without repelling good ones.
- **Visual identity as support, not substance**: Consistent framing and templates make existing proof legible; they don't substitute for having proof.
- **Monthly vs. quarterly review cadence**: Light tactical edits monthly (headline, CTA); strategic changes (niche, audience) only quarterly and only with evidence.
- **Evidence-gated repositioning**: Core positioning changes on a pattern across multiple data points, never on one bad call or one slow week.

## When to Use
- When writing or rewriting a LinkedIn headline, website hero, or bio — run it through the six-jobs checklist before publishing.
- When a lead is clearly a bad fit and you want language ready that repels similar leads before the call, not during it.
- Monthly, as a five-minute lead-quality check, even when things feel like they're going fine.
- Quarterly, when deciding whether to change audience, niche, or core promise — never on the strength of a single bad lead.
- When a referral or inbound message uses language that doesn't match your intended positioning — a sign your public copy is sending mixed signals.

## Common Mistakes
- Writing a bio as a CV summary (skills and years of experience) instead of naming an audience, outcome, and next step.
- Having anti-positioning boundaries only in your head, never written down or reflected anywhere public — so leads never self-filter.
- Stating boundaries with an attitude ("don't waste my time") that repels good-fit clients along with bad ones.
- Rewriting core positioning every few weeks based on the most recent lead or piece of feedback, instead of waiting for a real pattern.
