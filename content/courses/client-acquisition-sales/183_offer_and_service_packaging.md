# 183. Offer & Service Packaging

## What It Is
Clients don't buy "React, Node.js, PostgreSQL." They buy a business outcome with reduced uncertainty, and the way you communicate that is through packaging — turning a broad capability ("I can build custom software") into a small number of concrete, named offers a stranger can understand and choose between in under a minute. An unpackaged capability forces the client to do the translation work themselves — to imagine what "custom software development" would mean for their specific situation — and most of them won't bother; they'll ask a vaguely worded question instead ("can you build me an app?") that starts the relationship in the worst possible place: no shared understanding of scope.

A complete offer defines a name, target buyer, problem solved, deliverables, required client inputs, what's included, what's explicitly excluded, a typical timeline, and a starting CTA. The exclusions are not an afterthought — they're doing real acquisition work. "This MVP offer does not include native mobile apps, complex payment marketplace logic, or unlimited revisions unless separately scoped" repels the client who wants everything for one fixed low price, and it reassures the client who wants a professional, bounded engagement that you know how to say no. A small set of recognizable categories works well as a starting menu: technical discovery/product audit (for uncertain clients), SaaS MVP build (for founders), business workflow platform (for SMEs drowning in spreadsheets), admin panel/backoffice system, integration and automation sprint, and maintenance retainer. Each should be nameable in one sentence, which is also a filter — "custom software development" is not a service category; "SaaS MVP Build — auth, core workflows, database, deployment, handover, eight weeks" is.

For genuinely uncertain prospects — people who don't yet know what they need — a low-risk entry offer (a paid technical discovery, an MVP scope workshop, a codebase audit) does two jobs at once: it gives them a real, priced product to buy that doesn't require full project commitment, and it protects you from doing unpaid detailed planning disguised as "just a quick estimate." This is the single biggest structural defense against the most common failure mode for new freelancers: giving away hours of free scoping to unqualified prospects because there was no smaller thing to sell them first.

## Key Concepts
- **Packaged offer**: A named service with defined deliverables, exclusions, timeline, and CTA — not a vague capability statement.
- **Required offer fields**: Name, target buyer, problem solved, deliverables, client inputs required, exclusions, typical timeline, CTA.
- **Exclusions as a filtering tool**: Explicitly stating what's not included repels unrealistic-scope clients and reassures serious ones.
- **Core offer categories**: Technical discovery/audit, SaaS MVP build, workflow platform, admin panel, integration sprint, maintenance retainer.
- **Entry offer / low-risk first step**: A small, priced, low-commitment product (paid audit, scope workshop) for uncertain prospects, instead of free planning.
- **Outcome naming**: A good offer name states the outcome and rough scope in one line, not a generic category like "web development."
- **Service page structure**: Headline → problem → who it's for → what's included/excluded → process → proof → FAQ → CTA.

## Example Code

**Offer definition template** (fill one per service you sell):

```markdown
# Offer: <Concrete Name — e.g., "SaaS MVP Build">

## Best For
<Buyer and situation>

## Problem Solved
<The business pain, not the feature request>

## Outcome
<The business result the client walks away with>

## Deliverables
- <deliverable>
- <deliverable>

## Client Inputs Required
- <what you need from them to start>

## Explicitly Excluded
- <what a client might assume is included, but isn't, unless scoped separately>

## Typical Timeline
<range, framed as typical, never guaranteed>

## Starting CTA
<the one next action, e.g., "Request an MVP scope review">
```

**Three-tier packaging table** (helps clients compare tiers instead of comparing you to competitors):

```text
|              | Starter          | Growth              | Scale                  |
|--------------|-------------------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| What         | Scope + prototype | Full MVP build       | MVP + mobile companion  |
| Timeline     | 2 weeks           | 8 weeks              | 14 weeks                |
| Support      | 30-day bug fixes  | 60-day bug fixes     | 3-month retainer        |
```

## When to Use
- When you're repeatedly quoting the same type of project from scratch instead of pointing to a defined package.
- When a lead asks "can you build me an app" and you need something concrete to hand them instead of an open-ended question back.
- When designing a landing page, service page, or proposal template — the offer definition is the source content for all three.
- When a prospect is too uncertain to commit to a full build — offer the entry-level discovery/audit product instead of doing free planning.
- When raising rates — packaging around outcomes makes a price increase easier to justify than raising an hourly number.

## Common Mistakes
- Presenting a vague capability ("I can build any website or app you need") instead of a small number of named, scoped offers.
- Leaving exclusions unstated, which lets scope creep happen by default because nothing was ever ruled out.
- Doing detailed, unpaid scoping and planning for every inbound inquiry instead of selling a small entry offer first.
- Naming services after technical categories ("frontend," "backend," "DevOps") instead of buyer-facing outcomes.

## Further Reading
- *Value-Based Fees* — Alan Weiss: the foundational text on packaging services around outcomes rather than hours.
- *Hourly Billing Is Nuts* — Jonathan Stark: a short, direct case for productized, fixed-scope offers over open-ended hourly work.
- *The Win Without Pitching Manifesto* — Blair Enns: positioning and packaging discipline for independent technical and creative consultants.
