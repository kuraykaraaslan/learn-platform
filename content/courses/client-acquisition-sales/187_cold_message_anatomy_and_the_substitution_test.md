# 187. Cold Message Anatomy, Personalization & the Substitution Test

## What It Is
Every cold message, on every channel, follows the same four-part skeleton in the same fixed order: a specific researched fact, the consequence framed as a pattern rather than an accusation, one sentence of relevant capability tied to that consequence, and exactly one soft, low-cost question. The first message starts a conversation — it does not close a sale, book a meeting, or explain the whole service. When a draft runs over its length budget, the fix is almost always to cut part three, where "what I do" quietly turns into a service catalogue. The one-ask rule is absolute: one question, answerable with a yes or no, costing the recipient nothing to answer. A calendar link, a price, an attachment, or a second question in the first touch are all treated the same way — as asking a stranger for a favor before they've shown any interest.

The mechanism that actually separates a personalized message from a mail-merge is the substitution test: take the draft, swap in a different company's name and a different recipient's name, and check whether it still reads as sensible and true. If it does, it is not personalized — it is a template with a name inserted, and it fails on sight regardless of how much research theoretically went into producing it. A message that passes the test contains at least one sentence that would be false if sent to anyone else on the list. This is why "I noticed your company does great work" and "companies like yours often struggle with X" are banned outright — the token "your company" is the only thing distinguishing them from spam.

Research supports this discipline through three personalization tiers. T1 is the default: a specific, named, verifiable fact you found yourself, stated flatly and without flattery, with enough detail to prove you actually looked. T2 — segment-level framing shared across a list — is only acceptable for B or C tier prospects where the segment is genuinely narrow. T3, a fixed template with only the name and company swapped, is forbidden outright; inserting `<FirstName>` is not personalization, it is a mail-merge with manners. The evidence sentence itself follows a specific shape: it is falsifiable. "Your careers page has two backend roles open, both referencing the internal ordering tool" could be wrong, which is exactly what makes it evidence of having looked — a compliment about "innovative approach" could never be wrong, because it says nothing at all. Research has firm boundaries too: personal life, health, politics, private social accounts, and anything from a data breach are off-limits regardless of public availability, and referencing any of it doesn't just cross the line, it announces that you crossed it.

## Key Concepts
- **Four-part structure, fixed order**: specific context → consequence framed as a pattern → one capability sentence → one soft question.
- **The one-ask rule**: exactly one low-cost, yes/no-answerable question — no calendar link, no price, no attachment, no second ask in a first touch.
- **Length budgets by channel**: 70-110 words for email, 200-280 characters for a LinkedIn connection note, roughly 20 seconds for a phone opener — and part three is what to cut first when over budget.
- **The substitution test**: swap the company and recipient name; if the message still reads true, it is not personalized and must be rewritten or the prospect dropped.
- **Personalization tiers**: T1 (a specific verified fact, the default), T2 (narrow segment framing, B/C tier only), T3 (name-merge only — forbidden outright).
- **The evidence sentence is falsifiable**: a good opening line could theoretically be wrong; a compliment never can be, which is what makes it worthless as evidence of research.
- **Banned phrase list**: "just following up," "I hope this finds you well," "quick question," and similar filler mark a message as automated regardless of the research behind it.
- **Research has hard limits**: personal life, health, politics, private accounts, and breached data are off-limits no matter how publicly visible they happen to be.

## Example Code

**Four-part message skeleton:**

```text
1. Specific context:      <researched, falsifiable fact about this org>
2. Consequence (pattern): "Teams/companies at this stage usually find <problem>..."
3. Relevant capability:   <one sentence: what you do, tied directly to #2>
4. Soft question:         <one yes/no-answerable question, no calendar link>
```

**Substitution test worksheet:**

```markdown
Draft: <paste message>

Swap company name to: <a different prospect on the list>
Swap recipient name to: <a different recipient>

Does the message still read as sensible and true? Y / N
If Y — which sentence needs to become specific or falsifiable before this can send?
If N — the message passes; check the one-ask rule and length budget before sending.
```

## When to Use
- Writing any cold first touch, on any channel.
- Reviewing a drafted message before it goes out, especially one that felt fast or easy to write.
- Training your own eye to recognize generic outreach — both what you send and what lands in your own inbox.

## Common Mistakes
- Opening with who you are and what you do instead of what you found — a message that starts with you is about you.
- Asking two questions, or pairing the one question with a calendar link "just in case."
- Complimenting the company instead of stating a specific, falsifiable fact about it.
- Treating `<FirstName>` merge fields as personalization instead of what they actually are — a template.
- Researching private or personal information to make a message feel more "personal" than the professional facts actually support.

- *The Sales Development Playbook* — Trish Bertuzzi: message and cadence discipline at the rep level, scaled down to a solo operator's first touch.
