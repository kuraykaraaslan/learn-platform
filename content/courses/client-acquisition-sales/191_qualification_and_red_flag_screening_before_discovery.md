# 191. Qualification & Red-Flag Screening Before Discovery

## What It Is
Discovery calls are expensive, and the decision to book one deserves a real filter rather than a vibe check. The qualification model runs across six dimensions — need, authority, budget, timeline, fit, and risk — each rated strong, medium, weak, or unknown, and mapped to a concrete decision: book discovery, ask more first, offer a paid audit, nurture, or disqualify. The operational rule underneath it is specific and easy to apply consistently: a discovery call gets booked only when at least three of seven fields are already known — business problem, project type, must-have outcome, timeline, budget signal, decision maker, and current system. Fewer than three known means the next message is an intake request, not a calendar link, no matter how enthusiastic the prospect sounds.

Budget and authority are the two dimensions people flinch from asking about directly, so both get a scripted, non-apologetic way in. On budget: "I ask about budget early because it changes the responsible recommendation — the same problem can be solved as a lean MVP, a phased build, or a full platform, and without a range I might design the wrong level of solution." If the prospect refuses to engage with budget at all, the fallback isn't to push harder — it's to reframe the next step as a scope discussion rather than a price estimate, since scope is what a meaningful price would depend on anyway. On authority, if the person on the call clearly isn't the one who approves spending, the response asks to include that person directly, or offers a shareable summary instead of quietly proceeding as if authority weren't a variable.

Red flags run on a parallel track to fit-scoring, because a technically well-fitting lead can still carry serious risk. Five categories cover most of what actually goes wrong: payment risk (payment only after launch, no deposit, dodging budget conversation entirely), scope risk ("it's simple" attached to a complex platform, no must-have list, constant feature additions), authority risk (no one who can approve, visible internal disagreement), communication risk (rude tone, disappearing repeatedly, pressuring for an instant estimate), and ethical/legal risk (scraping, phishing-like flows, credential theft, anything illegal). Each category has its own calm scripted response rather than an improvised defense, and the response is diagnostic, not accusatory — it names the risk without shaming the person raising it. Disqualification itself isn't one flat action; it has four levels — soft (not ready now, nurture), commercial (budget/timeline mismatch, suggest smaller scope or decline), process (refuses scope, deposit, or written terms — decline), and ethical (unsafe or illegal request — refuse outright) — and matching the level to the actual situation keeps a budget mismatch from being handled with the same blunt language an ethical refusal deserves.

## Key Concepts
- **Six-dimension qualification model**: Need, Authority, Budget, Timeline, Fit, Risk — each rated strong/medium/weak/unknown, mapped to a specific next action.
- **The call-booking rule**: book a discovery call only when at least 3 of 7 fields are known (problem, project type, must-have outcome, timeline, budget signal, decision maker, current system); fewer than 3 means send an intake request first.
- **Budget framing script**: ask directly and explain why it changes the recommendation, with a scope-discussion fallback when the prospect refuses to engage on budget at all.
- **Authority handling**: when the contact clearly isn't the approver, ask to include the actual decision maker rather than proceeding as though authority doesn't matter.
- **Five red-flag categories**: payment, scope, authority, communication, and ethical/legal risk — each with its own signal list and a calm, non-accusatory scripted response.
- **Four disqualification levels**: soft (nurture) / commercial (mismatch — shrink or decline) / process (refuses terms — decline) / ethical (unsafe — refuse outright) — matched to the actual situation, not applied uniformly.
- **Politeness is not buying intent**: a friendly, agreeable prospect with an unknown budget and no named decision maker is still an unqualified lead.
- **Never proposal from a red flag**: repeated red-flag behavior overrides an otherwise-promising fit score.

## Example Code

**Qualification scorecard:**

```markdown
Need:      strong / medium / weak
Authority: known / unknown / weak
Budget:    clear / signal only / unknown / poor
Timeline:  clear / flexible / unknown
Fit:       strong / medium / weak
Risk:      low / medium / high

Decision: Book discovery / Ask more context / Offer paid audit / Nurture / Disqualify
Reason:
Next message:
```

**Red-flag quick check:**

```text
[ ] Payment risk    — payment only after launch, no deposit, avoids budget talk
[ ] Scope risk      — "it's simple" + complex platform, no must-have list
[ ] Authority risk  — cannot name an approver, visible internal disagreement
[ ] Communication risk — rude tone, disappears, pressures for instant estimate
[ ] Ethical/legal risk — scraping, phishing-like flow, illegal functionality

2+ checked -> escalate to disqualification level: soft / commercial / process / ethical
```

## When to Use
- Right before deciding whether to book a discovery call with a new prospect.
- The moment any red-flag signal appears during intake or an early exchange.
- Deciding whether continuing with a lead costs more time and risk than it's worth.
- Writing a decline message that needs to stay professional and protect the relationship.

## Common Mistakes
- Booking a call the moment someone replies, regardless of how many of the seven fields are actually known.
- Treating polite, friendly communication as a proxy for real buying intent or authority.
- Giving a detailed free plan to "prove value" before any qualification has happened.
- Continuing with a prospect after two or more red flags have already appeared.
- Using the same blunt decline language for a simple budget mismatch as for an ethical refusal — they call for different tones.

## Further Reading
- *SPIN Selling* — Neil Rackham: structured need-diagnosis questioning that underlies the "Need" dimension of this model.
- *Getting to Yes* — Roger Fisher & William Ury: separating the person from the problem, useful when a red-flag conversation risks turning adversarial.
- *The Trusted Advisor* — David Maister, Charles Green & Robert Galford: earning the standing to say no to a bad-fit client without damaging the relationship.
