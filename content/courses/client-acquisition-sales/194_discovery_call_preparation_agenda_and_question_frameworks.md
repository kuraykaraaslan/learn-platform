# 194. Discovery Call Preparation, Agenda & Question Frameworks

## What It Is
A discovery call is the first stage that starts after acquisition and qualification are done — the prospect has already passed the filters in earlier lessons, and now the job shifts from "is this worth a conversation" to "what exactly is this project." That shift fails constantly for one reason: showing up with no structure and letting the client's energy define the call, which usually means racing straight into technology, features, and price before anyone understands the business problem. Preparation exists to prevent that. A one-page brief before the call — known problem, known signals, likely stakeholders, possible risks, and the questions still open — turns the call from an improvised conversation into the execution of a plan, and a pre-call hypothesis ("they likely need X because Y, the main risk may be Z, the decision likely depends on W") gives the call something concrete to confirm or correct rather than discover from zero.

The agenda that runs during the call follows one fixed shape regardless of whether it's thirty minutes or forty-five: open, frame, diagnose, clarify, constrain, summarize, next step. Framing happens in the first five minutes with a script that sets expectations explicitly — "the goal today is not to jump straight into a price or technology choice" — because a client who isn't told the process will supply their own, usually a rushed one. The middle of the call belongs to a small set of control phrases for the three ways clients reliably try to hijack it: jumping to features ("let me first understand the workflow it supports"), demanding price too early ("I can talk about budget ranges after we clarify the must-have scope"), and pushing for implementation detail before scope exists ("the right approach depends on users, data, integrations, and launch expectations"). None of these phrases refuse the client anything — they redirect back to the sequence without becoming adversarial.

Underneath both preparation and agenda sits the question framework that actually produces useful answers: a fixed layering of why → who → current state → desired state → scope → constraints → decision → risk, deliberately never starting with technology unless the client's problem is already technically defined. Ten categories fill in that layering — business goal, users, current workflow, desired workflow, must-have scope, data and integrations, timeline, budget, decision process, and risk — and each has a small bank of concrete questions plus follow-ups ("can you give me an example," "who does that step," "what would be unacceptable"). The framework also names its own failure mode: vague, feel-good questions like "what's your dream app" or "do you want it modern" produce vague, unusable answers no matter how many of them get asked, and a forty-question form sent before any trust exists does more damage than a focused set of seven to twelve.

## Key Concepts
- **The pre-call brief and hypothesis**: known problem, signals, stakeholders, and risks written down beforehand, plus an explicit "they likely need X because Y, the risk may be Z" hypothesis to confirm or correct — never walking in blind.
- **Fixed agenda shape**: open → frame → diagnose → clarify → constrain → summarize → next step, whether the call is 30 or 45 minutes.
- **The framing script**: stated explicitly in the first five minutes, so the client knows this call is not a price quote or a tech-stack discussion.
- **Agenda control phrases**: specific redirects for premature features, premature price, and premature implementation detail — redirect, don't refuse.
- **Layered question framework**: why → who → current state → desired state → scope → constraints → decision → risk, never opening with technology unless the client already brought a defined technical problem.
- **Ten question categories**: business goal, users, current workflow, desired workflow, must-have scope, data/integrations, timeline, budget, decision process, risk — each with concrete, example-based questions.
- **Bad-question pattern**: "what features do you want," "do you want it modern" — replace with specific, falsifiable questions like "what would make the first version fail in your eyes."
- **The question-count ceiling**: 7-12 focused questions before trust exists; deeper questionnaires are reserved for paid discovery, not the first call.

## Example Code

**Pre-call brief and hypothesis template:**

```markdown
Prospect:            
Company:             
Lead source:         
Known problem:       
Known signals:       (budget/timeline/authority)
Likely stakeholders: 
Possible risks:      

Hypothesis: They likely need <project type> because <business trigger>.
The main risk may be <risk>.
The decision likely depends on <budget / timeline / authority / value>.

Call goals (3-5):
1.
2.
3.
```

**45-minute agenda skeleton:**

```text
0-5 min    Frame     — confirm purpose and process
5-15 min   Diagnose  — business problem, why now
15-25 min  Clarify   — users, current workflow, desired workflow
25-32 min  Scope     — must-have vs later
32-38 min  Constrain — timeline, budget, stakeholders
38-43 min  Summarize — repeat back understanding
43-45 min  Next step — proposal / paid discovery / follow-up / no fit
```

## When to Use
- Before every discovery call, intro call, or technical scoping conversation with a prospect who has already been qualified.
- Building a reusable call-prep template and agenda so quality doesn't depend on how alert you feel that day.
- When a client keeps steering the conversation toward price or tech stack before scope exists.
- Drafting or auditing an intake form or question set to make sure it produces decisions, not just data.

## Common Mistakes
- Walking into a call with no brief and no hypothesis, letting the client's framing define the entire conversation.
- Opening with a long personal introduction or capability pitch instead of stating the process up front.
- Answering every feature tangent as it comes up instead of redirecting back to workflow and scope.
- Sending a long, generic intake form before any trust has been established.
- Ending a call without a stated next step, leaving both sides to guess what happens next.

## Further Reading
- *Rainmaking Conversations* — Mike Schultz & John E. Doerr: structuring a consultative sales conversation around a repeatable question flow instead of an improvised pitch.
- *Questions That Sell* — Paul Cherry: building question sets that surface real business value instead of a feature wishlist.
- *Just Listen* — Mark Goulston: getting a guarded, rushed, or skeptical prospect to actually open up inside a short call.
