# 277. Content Calendar, Cadence & the Pre-Publish Quality Gate

## What It Is
A content calendar's job is to create consistency without turning into a second job — which means the biggest design mistake is planning more than a solo operator can realistically sustain alongside actual client work. The right cadence is the one that survives a busy delivery week, not the one that looks most impressive on paper. A workable default for a solo technical business is two LinkedIn posts a week, one short technical or proof post, one weekly idea-capture/review session, and one repurposing action — with a blog every two to four weeks, a monthly newsletter, and a portfolio or case-study update once a month or per completed project layered on only once that base cadence feels sustainable.

Cadence should flex with capacity rather than being a fixed commitment that gets broken and abandoned. A useful way to think about it is three named levels: a minimum-sustainable mode (one LinkedIn post a week, one capture session, one proof update a month) for busy delivery periods; a professional-consistency mode (two posts a week, one blog a month, one newsletter a month, one repurposed asset a week) as the recommended default; and a growth-push mode (three posts a week, a blog every two weeks, weekly video, active networking) reserved for two-to-three-week bursts around a launch or acquisition push, not sustained indefinitely. The missed-week rule matters here: if a week gets skipped, the fix is publishing the next useful piece and continuing, not restarting the whole system or feeling obligated to catch up on missed volume.

Before anything gets published, it should pass a five-gate quality check: strategy (does it map to a pillar and a business goal?), clarity (is the main point understandable in one sentence, and does the hook explain why the reader should care?), specificity (is there a concrete example, or could any developer have published the exact same generic content?), trust (does it avoid exaggerated claims, respect confidentiality, and demonstrate real judgment?), and conversion (is there a natural next step, even if it's just an implicit one?). Not every post needs a hard CTA, but every post needs a strategic reason to exist — content that fails the strategy gate should be revised or discarded regardless of how polished the writing is.

## Key Concepts
- **Buffer rule**: maintain roughly five raw ideas, three drafted posts, and one ready-to-publish post at all times — this buffer is what prevents inconsistent posting during heavy delivery weeks.
- **Three cadence levels**: minimum-sustainable (survival mode during busy periods), professional-consistency (the recommended steady-state default), and growth-push (a temporary, higher-intensity mode for launches, used for weeks not months).
- **Missed-week rule**: if a week is skipped, resume with the next useful piece of content rather than trying to "catch up" or restarting the entire system from scratch.
- **Platform priority, not platform equality**: pick one primary channel (typically LinkedIn for a B2B technical practice), one secondary (website/blog), a proof layer (portfolio/GitHub/demos), and a nurture layer (newsletter) — don't spread equal effort across five platforms at once.
- **The five publish gates**: strategy, clarity, specificity, trust, and conversion — a piece that fails any gate gets revised or discarded before it goes out, regardless of how much time was already invested in it.
- **The specificity test**: "could another developer publish this exact content unchanged?" — if yes, it's too generic to publish as-is.
- **Weekly theme rotation**: assigning a recurring theme to each publishing day (e.g., Monday = client education, Wednesday = engineering quality, Friday = proof/lesson) removes the daily decision of "what do I post about" and keeps the pillar mix balanced automatically.
- **Calendar fields that matter**: date, platform, pillar, audience, format, hook/title, status, CTA, repurposing source, and success signal — enough structure to review what's working without becoming an editorial bureaucracy.

## Example Code
```template
## Weekly Content Calendar

**Business goal:** Generate qualified inbound project inquiries
**Primary audience:** SME owners and founders
**Cadence level:** Professional Consistency

| Day | Platform | Pillar | Format | Hook | CTA | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | LinkedIn | Client Education | Text post | "Before asking for a quote, prepare these 5 things" | Send workflow before requesting a price | Scheduled |
| Wed | LinkedIn | Engineering Quality | Text post | "A dashboard isn't finished until roles and logs exist" | Implicit (soft) | Drafting |
| Fri | LinkedIn | Proof | Case-study snippet | "How I scoped a role-based appointment system" | Review architecture / start a project review | Raw |

**Buffer check:** 5 raw ideas ✓ | 3 drafted ✓ | 1 ready-to-publish ✓

---

## Pre-Publish Quality Gate

**Content title/hook:** "Before asking for a quote, prepare these 5 things"

- [x] Strategy — maps to Client Education pillar, supports lead quality goal
- [x] Clarity — main point is understandable in one sentence
- [x] Specificity — includes a concrete example (booking system scope gap)
- [x] Trust — no exaggerated claims, no confidential detail
- [x] Conversion — soft CTA present, natural next step for a problem-aware reader

**Publish decision:** Publish
```

## When to Use
- When setting up a content practice for the first time and needing a cadence that will actually survive real client work
- When content has become inconsistent — disappearing for weeks then posting in bursts — and needs a sustainable floor to fall back to
- Before every single publish, as a two-minute gate check rather than an occasional audit
- When promoting a launch, new service, or major case study and temporarily increasing output for a defined two-to-three-week window
- When reviewing whether a content habit is realistic against actual weekly capacity, not aspirational capacity

## Common Mistakes
- **The plan calls for a daily post across four platforms, and it collapses the first week a client project gets busy** — Designing a daily, multi-platform posting plan that looks aggressive on paper but collapses within two weeks of real client work
- **One missed week turns into the whole content habit quietly stopping instead of just resuming with the next post** — Treating a missed week as a reason to abandon the whole system instead of simply resuming with the next piece
- **A generic, unspecific post goes out anyway, just to keep the streak alive under a busy week's time pressure** — Skipping the quality gate under time pressure and publishing generic, unspecific content just to "keep the streak going"
- **Effort gets split evenly across LinkedIn, Twitter, a blog, YouTube, and a newsletter, with none of them getting enough attention to actually work** — Spreading effort evenly across many platforms instead of committing to one primary channel and treating the rest as secondary or optional
