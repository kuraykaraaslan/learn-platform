# 288. Landing Page, Local and Service SEO

## What It Is
A landing or service page carries commercial intent, so it has a harder job than a blog post: it needs to rank *and* convert a qualified visitor, and ranking without converting wastes the traffic entirely. Every service page needs to answer, in order: who is this for, what problem does it solve, what outcome does it create, what's included, what's excluded (boundaries matter as much as inclusions — vague scope creates buyer hesitation and later disputes), what proof exists, what's the process, and what should the visitor do next. The recommended structure — hero with service/audience/outcome, problem section, outcome/value section, inclusions, exclusions, process, proof/case studies, FAQ, CTA — exists specifically to front-load conversion signals instead of burying the CTA under generic "modern, scalable" copy that could describe any developer's page. A common failure is stuffing every service the business offers onto one generic "Services" page; separate, specific service pages ("Custom SaaS MVP Development," "Custom Admin Panel Development") each rank for their own commercial queries in a way one catch-all page never can.

Local and service SEO is a smaller, more specific problem: helping the right buyers understand where the business actually operates — remote, local, or both — without misrepresenting anything. The rule that matters most here is truthfulness: accurate service-area language ("I work remotely with SMEs, founders, and agencies in Turkey and internationally") is fine; implying a physical office in a city where none exists is not, and it's also a fast way to get flagged by search engines that specifically watch for fake local signals. If local pages are created at all, each one needs genuinely unique value — local context, region-specific case studies, real contact details — because a set of city pages that only swap the city name ("software developer Istanbul," "software developer Ankara," "software developer Izmir") with otherwise identical copy is a textbook doorway-page pattern that search engines actively suppress. For most solo technical businesses, specific service pages should be built first, with local targeting layered on only where it reflects an actual, differentiated business reality — not created reflexively because "local SEO" sounds like a checkbox to tick.

Directory and profile consistency threads through both: name, website, service description, and profile links should match across LinkedIn, GitHub, any Google Business-style profile, freelance directories, and agency partner pages, because inconsistent business identity weakens the same authority and trust signals that entity clarity work is trying to build elsewhere.

## Key Concepts
- **Commercial-page question set**: who is this for, what problem, what outcome, what's included, what's excluded, what proof, what process, what's next — all required, in that order.
- **One service, one page**: a catch-all "Services" page competing for every commercial query at once loses to focused, specific service pages every time.
- **Boundaries are a conversion tool, not a limitation**: explicitly stating what's *not* included reduces buyer hesitation and mismatched expectations more than it costs in scope.
- **Truthful service-area language**: describe remote/local reality accurately; never imply a physical presence that doesn't exist.
- **Local pages need genuine unique value**: real regional context and proof, not a template with the city name swapped — the latter is a doorway-page pattern search engines suppress.
- **Service pages before local pages**: build specific, well-differentiated service pages first; add local targeting only when it reflects real business strategy.
- **Directory consistency**: matching name, description, and links across LinkedIn, GitHub, and any local/business profiles reinforces the same trust signals as entity clarity work elsewhere.
- **Trust proof on commercial pages**: case studies, stack, screenshots, process explanation, testimonials, and demo/GitHub links all reduce the risk a prospect feels before contacting.

## Example Code
```md
## Service Page Structure

Hero: Custom SaaS MVP Development for SMEs and Founders
Problem: Manual processes and unscoped ideas turn into expensive builds
Outcome: A production-ready MVP that proves the product without overbuilding
What's included: discovery, architecture, auth, core workflow, deployment
What's not included: mobile app (phase 2), advanced analytics (roadmap)
Process: Discovery → Scope → Build → Test → Deploy → Handover
Proof: [case study link] [demo video]
FAQ: "How long does an MVP take?" / "What affects cost?"
CTA: Request a scope review

## Local/Service Language Check

Truthful (remote-first):
"I work remotely with SMEs, founders, and agencies in Turkey and internationally."

Truthful (local-eligible):
"Freelance software developer in Turkey focused on SaaS MVPs, admin panels,
and business workflow platforms."

Avoid: implying a physical office that does not exist; copying one page
across "software developer Istanbul / Ankara / Izmir" with no unique content.
```

## When to Use
- When building any page whose primary job is converting a qualified visitor, not just informing a reader
- When a "Services" page is trying to rank for many unrelated commercial queries at once and underperforming on all of them
- When deciding whether creating city- or region-specific pages is worth doing, versus a truthful remote-first positioning
- When auditing directory listings (LinkedIn, GitHub, freelance platforms) for name and description consistency
- Before publishing any page that states or implies a service area, to confirm the wording matches business reality

## Common Mistakes
- Combining every service into one generic page that ranks for nothing specific
- Omitting what's excluded from a service description, leaving scope boundaries to be discovered later in a dispute
- Implying a local office or presence that doesn't actually exist
- Publishing near-identical city pages with only the city name changed
- Letting business name or description drift across LinkedIn, GitHub, and directory profiles
