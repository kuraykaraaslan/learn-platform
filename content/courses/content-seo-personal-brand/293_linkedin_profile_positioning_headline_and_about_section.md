# 293. LinkedIn Profile Positioning, Headline and About Section

## What It Is
A LinkedIn profile has one job in the first few seconds a visitor spends on it: making the visitor instantly understand who you help, what problem you solve, what you build, why you're credible, and what to do next. Most technical profiles fail this test by leading with a generic stack list ("Full-stack developer | React | Node.js | PostgreSQL") — technically accurate, and completely uninformative about who should care. The fix is a positioning formula: "I help \<target client\> achieve \<outcome\> by building \<solution category\> with \<method/proof\>," which forces the profile to name a buyer and an outcome before it ever gets to the stack. The message hierarchy that should follow from this is deliberate: business outcome first, types of systems built second, delivery credibility third, technical stack fourth, proof/portfolio fifth, call to action last — leading with the stack instead of the outcome is the single most common positioning mistake on technical LinkedIn profiles.

The headline is the highest-leverage 220 characters on the entire profile because it's what appears next to every comment, every post, and every search result before anyone clicks through — it needs to include at least three of target client, software category, business outcome, credibility/method, and core stack, using a pattern like "I help \<target\> build \<software category\> for \<outcome\> | \<stack or method\>." The About section then has to convert the click the headline earned: who I help, what problem I solve, what I build, how I work, proof/credibility, and how to contact me, written in first person, with the buyer and their problem named in the first two lines — because those two lines are all that shows before a reader has to click "see more," and if they don't make sense standing alone, most readers never click through to the rest.

Two failure patterns show up constantly and are worth naming directly. The first is writing a life story or biography before ever stating the offer — buyers don't need the origin story before they need to know if you can solve their problem. The second is defaulting to a job-seeker tone ("Software engineer passionate about technology," "React developer looking for opportunities") when the actual goal is client acquisition — this reads as available-for-hire in the employee sense, not as a business a company would engage. Positioning anchors — recurring phrases like "production-ready," "clean architecture," "workflow automation," "technical delivery partner" — should show up consistently across the profile, posts, DMs, and even proposals, because that repetition is what eventually makes the freelancer memorable and recognizable rather than forgettable.

## Key Concepts
- **The positioning formula**: "I help \<target client\> achieve \<outcome\> by building \<solution category\> with \<method/proof\>" — names a buyer and outcome before the stack.
- **Message hierarchy order**: business outcome → types of systems built → delivery credibility → technical stack → proof/portfolio → CTA.
- **Headline as highest-leverage real estate**: it appears next to every comment and post; it needs at least three of target client, software category, outcome, credibility, and stack.
- **The About section's five-part structure**: who I help, what problem I solve, what I build, how I work, proof/credibility, then a contact CTA.
- **The first-two-lines test**: the About section's opening must make sense without clicking "see more," since most readers never expand it.
- **Positioning anchors**: recurring specific phrases ("production-ready," "clean architecture," "technical delivery partner") reused consistently across profile, posts, and DMs to build recognizability.
- **Anti-pattern: stack-first identity**: "Full-stack developer | React | Node.js" is accurate but says nothing about who should care or why.
- **Anti-pattern: job-seeker tone**: "passionate problem solver," "looking for opportunities" reads as available-for-hire, not as a business a company would engage.

## Example Code
```md
## Headline Formula

I help <target> build <software category> for <outcome> | <stack/method>

Example:
I help SMEs & founders build production-ready SaaS MVPs, admin panels
and web platforms | React, Next.js, Node.js, PostgreSQL

## About Section Skeleton

I help SMEs, founders, and agencies turn business workflows, SaaS ideas,
and internal operations into production-ready web software.

Most clients do not only need "a website." They need a system that
works: clear scope, reliable architecture, secure authentication, clean
UI, backend APIs, database design, deployment, and handover documentation.

I usually work on:
- SaaS MVPs
- Admin panels and dashboards
- Workflow automation platforms

My approach:
1. Understand the business problem
2. Separate MVP from future roadmap
3. Design a maintainable technical foundation
4. Build, test, deploy, and document the system

If your company is planning a custom web platform, send me a message
with a short description of the problem and current workflow.
```

## When to Use
- When setting up or rewriting a LinkedIn profile for the first time as a positioning exercise, not just a resume update
- When a profile currently leads with a technology stack and needs to be re-ordered around buyer outcome
- When the headline hasn't been touched in months and no longer reflects current positioning or service focus
- After a niche or service change, to check whether the profile's language has caught up to the new positioning
- When drafting proposals or DMs, to reuse the same positioning anchors established on the profile

## Common Mistakes
- **A profile headline opens with a stack list: "React | Node.js | AWS"** — Leading the profile with a stack list instead of the buyer and the outcome
- **The About section opens with personal biography before ever saying what problem you solve, for whom** — Writing a personal biography before ever stating what problem is solved for whom
- **A profile written for client acquisition uses phrases like "passionate" and "looking for opportunities"** — Defaulting to job-seeker language ("passionate," "looking for opportunities") when the actual goal is client acquisition
- **The skills section lists every technology you've ever touched** — Listing every technology ever touched instead of the handful that matter to the target buyer
- **Your positioning changed months ago, but the headline never did** — Letting the headline go stale after a positioning or service change instead of treating it as a living asset
