## What It Is
The welcome sequence is the first automated relationship a subscriber has with the business, and its job is narrow: deliver on the promise, set expectations, and create one low-pressure opening for conversation — not sell. A minimum 3-email version delivers the lead magnet and explains what's coming next in email one, names the mistake or problem the audience commonly faces in email two, and shows proof of how the freelancer actually approaches projects while inviting a qualified reply in email three. A fuller 5-email version stretches this across roughly ten days: welcome and delivery on day 0, why software projects fail before code on day 2, MVP versus roadmap on day 4, case study or technical proof on day 7, and a soft CTA to reply, book, or request a review on day 10. Selling aggressively in email one is explicitly forbidden — the entire sequence exists to earn the right to ask for anything, and skipping straight to the ask undoes that in the first message.

Beyond the welcome sequence, ongoing nurture sequences exist for anyone who isn't ready to buy yet but shouldn't just be left in the general newsletter either — someone who downloaded a specific resource, attended a call that didn't close, or paused a project. The design starts by identifying which of five buying-stage mindsets the subscriber is actually in: problem-aware ("this process is painful"), solution-aware ("we may need software"), provider-aware ("could this freelancer help?"), decision-ready ("should we talk?"), or not-now ("maybe later"). Each stage needs a different email goal — diagnosing and educating a problem-aware reader is a different job than inviting a decision-ready one to reply — and sending the same generic sequence to everyone regardless of stage is one of the most common ways nurture quietly stops working.

A useful default is a 6-email arc: the cost of unclear scope, how to map the first workflow, MVP versus full platform, what a serious technical partner actually checks, a case study or before-after example, and a soft invitation to a project review. Spacing depends on how warm the list is — 2 to 4 days between emails for an active lead-magnet sequence, 7 to 14 days for slower nurture, and monthly for long-term relationship maintenance. Sequences should also have named exit conditions: a contact should leave or switch sequences the moment they book a call, reply with real project details, request a proposal, become a client, unsubscribe, or go inactive after repeated non-engagement. A sequence with no exit condition just keeps running past the point where it's still useful, which is how automated nurture starts to feel robotic even when every individual email was well-written.

## Key Concepts
- **Welcome sequence goal**: deliver the promised asset, set expectations for future emails, and invite a low-pressure reply — never a hard pitch in email one.
- **3-email minimum vs. 5-email expanded welcome**: delivery-and-expectations → problem framing → proof-and-next-step, optionally stretched with a dedicated MVP-vs-roadmap email and spread across roughly ten days.
- **Five buying-stage mindsets**: problem-aware, solution-aware, provider-aware, decision-ready, and not-now — each needs its own email goal, not a shared generic sequence.
- **Default 6-email nurture arc**: unclear scope cost → workflow mapping → MVP vs. platform → what a serious partner checks → proof/case study → soft invitation.
- **Sequence-specific goals**: an MVP-planning sequence, a workflow-automation sequence, and an agency-partner sequence each need a distinct, explicitly stated goal before writing the first email.
- **Spacing by sequence type**: 2–4 days for active lead-magnet sequences, 7–14 days for slower nurture, monthly for long-term relationship maintenance.
- **Named exit conditions**: books a call, replies with details, requests a proposal, becomes a client, unsubscribes, or goes inactive — a sequence without exit conditions keeps running past its usefulness.
- **One question per email**: every nurture email should answer "what does the reader need to understand before the next step feels natural?" — not try to close the deal on its own.

## Welcome Sequence (5-email version)

Day 2:  Why software projects fail before code (problem framing).
Day 4:  How to separate MVP from roadmap.
Day 7:  Case study or technical proof.
Day 10: Soft CTA to reply / book / request a project review.

## When to Use
- Immediately after building a lead magnet or opt-in, before the first subscriber ever joins
- When a new audience segment (agency partners, past clients, MVP founders) needs its own path instead of the general sequence
- When a sales cycle has stalled and a subscriber needs a clearly defined next stage to move toward
- When auditing why an existing sequence isn't converting — usually a missing stage match or a missing exit condition
- When a lead magnet download or paused conversation needs a defined re-entry path rather than silence

## Common Mistakes
- Sending the same generic sequence to every subscriber regardless of buying-stage mindset
- Opening the welcome sequence with a hard sales pitch instead of delivery and expectation-setting
- Leaving a sequence with no exit condition, so contacts keep receiving irrelevant emails after they've already converted or gone cold
- Over-automating a conversation that a genuine reply deserves to receive personally
- Promising a cadence (e.g., daily emails) the sequence design can't actually sustain
