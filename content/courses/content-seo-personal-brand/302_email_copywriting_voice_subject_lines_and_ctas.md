# 302. Email Copywriting — Voice, Subject Lines & CTAs

## What It Is
The email voice for a technical solo business has a narrow target: it should read like a serious consultant writing to business-minded people — professional, plain-language, specific, confident but not arrogant, and low-pressure. The single most useful writing constraint is one email, one idea; covering five topics in one send dilutes all of them and gives the reader nothing specific to react to. The opening line does the most work: "Many software projects become expensive because the first version tries to include every future idea" earns attention because it names a recognizable problem, while "I recently learned a new technology" earns nothing because it's about the writer instead of the reader. Concrete examples consistently beat abstract advice — "define the one workflow that proves the business case" is forgettable, but walking through exactly which features a ticketing system's first version needs (event listing, pricing, checkout, QR ticket, admin panel) versus which wait (loyalty points, mobile app, analytics) gives the reader something they can actually apply. A small phrase bank reinforces the voice consistently: "the responsible first step is," "this usually fails when," "before estimating, clarify" all sound like the same credible person, while "game changer," "crush it," and "10x your business overnight" sound like a template nobody wrote personally.

Subject lines and preheaders are the gate the rest of the email has to earn its way through, and the standard is specific, truthful, short, and connected to the reader's actual problem — curiosity-based is fine, clickbait is not, because a subject line that oversells what's inside spends trust the email itself can't repay. Four subject line types cover most situations: problem-based ("Your MVP probably has too many first-version features"), framework-based ("A simple way to separate MVP from roadmap"), proof-based ("How a manual workflow becomes an internal platform"), and reply-based ("What are you trying to automate?"). The preheader's job is to complete the subject rather than repeat it — if the subject names the five questions asked before estimating software, the preheader explains what those questions reveal (scope, budget risk, technical unknowns) rather than restating "five questions I ask."

The CTA is where all of this either converts into a conversation or doesn't, and the operating rule is one primary CTA per email, chosen to match the reader's stage rather than defaulting to the same ask everywhere. A new subscriber gets invited to reply with context or read an asset; a decision-ready lead gets invited to book discovery or send an intake form — using "book a call" as the CTA for someone who just joined the list skips several stages of trust that haven't been earned yet. Most nurture emails should carry a soft CTA ("Reply with one sentence about your current process") and reserve hard CTAs ("Book a paid discovery call") for once enough context has actually been built. And the loop only closes if replies are treated as the valuable signal they are — when someone replies with real project context, that's the moment to pause automation, read carefully, and respond personally, not the moment to let a generic sequence keep firing past a live conversation.

## Key Concepts
- **One email, one idea**: resist covering multiple topics in a single send — pick one insight and make it useful rather than diluting several.
- **Problem-first opening**: start with the reader's pain or a recognizable mistake, not with what the writer recently learned or did.
- **Concrete beats abstract**: a specific worked example (exact features in vs. out for a real project type) is more persuasive and more memorable than general advice.
- **Preferred phrase bank vs. hype language**: "the responsible first step is," "this is a scope risk, not only a technical issue" sound consistent and credible; "game changer" and "10x overnight" sound like generic funnel copy.
- **Four subject line types**: problem-based, framework-based, proof-based, and reply-based — all specific, truthful, and tied to the reader's problem, never clickbait.
- **Preheader completes, doesn't repeat**: the preheader should add the payoff the subject line implies, not restate the subject in different words.
- **CTA matched to stage**: new subscribers get a reply-or-read CTA; decision-ready leads get a discovery-or-intake CTA — using the same CTA everywhere ignores how much trust has actually been built.
- **Soft CTA as the default, hard CTA as the exception**: most nurture emails should invite a low-pressure reply; a hard "book a call" ask belongs only once enough context exists to justify it.

## Example Code
```md
## Email Skeleton (5-part structure)

1. Problem or observation
2. Why it matters (business terms)
3. Practical framework or example
4. Short point of view
5. Soft CTA or reply prompt

## Subject / Preheader Pair

Subject:   The 5 questions I ask before estimating software
Preheader: They reveal scope, budget risk, technical unknowns, and whether
           a proposal is even responsible yet.

## CTA Selection by Stage

| Stage             | Best CTA                                    |
|-------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| New subscriber    | Reply with context, or read an asset          |
| Problem-aware      | Reply with the current workflow problem       |
| Solution-aware     | Read a case study or checklist                |
| Decision-ready     | Book discovery / send intake                  |
| Past client        | Ask about improvement or maintenance          |

## Good vs. Weak Opening

Weak:   "I recently learned a new technology."
Strong: "Many software projects become expensive because the first version
         tries to include every future idea."
```

## When to Use
- Drafting any newsletter, nurture, or welcome email before sending it
- Choosing a subject line for an important sequence — generate several variants and select on clarity and specificity, not novelty
- Reviewing a draft that feels flat, to check whether it's covering one idea or several at once
- When replies have stopped coming in, to check whether the CTA matches the reader's actual stage
- Immediately after receiving a high-intent reply, to decide the response deserves a personal answer rather than an automated one

## Common Mistakes
- Opening with a personal update or biography instead of the reader's problem
- Writing a subject line that oversells what the email actually delivers
- Stacking multiple CTAs into one email so the reader isn't sure which action matters
- Defaulting to "book a call" as the CTA for subscribers who haven't built enough context to want that yet
- Letting a generic automated response go out in reply to a meaningful, high-intent message
