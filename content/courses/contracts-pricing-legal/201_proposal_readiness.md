# 201. Proposal Readiness — Knowing When You're Ready to Quote

## What It Is
The single most common cause of underpriced, over-scoped, resentment-generating projects is writing a proposal before you actually understand what you're proposing. A prospect says "I need an app like Uber but for dog walking" and a nervous freelancer, afraid of losing the lead, sends back a number within a day. That number is fiction — not because the freelancer is bad at estimating, but because there is nothing yet to estimate. Proposal readiness is the discipline of refusing to write a binding commercial document until a specific, minimum set of facts is known.

The readiness bar isn't "perfect clarity." It's a short checklist: the business problem, the desired outcome, the must-have workflows for a first version, who the users are, what already exists, a timeline expectation, a budget signal, and who actually has authority to say yes. If those are missing, the correct move is not to guess — it's to redirect the conversation toward discovery, a paid audit, or a short scoping call, and to say so plainly rather than quietly padding the number to cover the uncertainty.

Readiness exists on a gradient, not a binary. A prospect who says "can you make a website?" is at the bottom — send intake questions, don't estimate. A prospect who has a clear problem, a named decision-maker, and a rough budget range is close to the top — you can move straight to a written proposal. Most real conversations sit in the middle, and the freelancer's job is to diagnose which level they're at before doing any pricing work, because a proposal written from Level 0 information is a liability disguised as a sales tool.

This lesson is a discipline lesson, not a legal one — but the habits here directly protect the legal and commercial boundaries covered later in this course. A proposal built on real information is much easier to convert cleanly into a Statement of Work, because the two documents describe the same reality instead of two different projects.

## Key Concepts
- **Readiness gate**: a proposal should not be written until business problem, outcome, must-have scope, users, starting point, timeline, budget signal, and decision-maker are all known.
- **Readiness levels**: Not Ready (vague idea) → Light Context → Discovery Ready → Proposal Ready — each level has a different correct next action, not just a different price.
- **Budget signal**: some indication of what's been allocated or is realistic — without it, a fixed number is a guess dressed as precision.
- **Decision-maker identification**: knowing who can actually approve the deal before investing time writing to please the wrong person.
- **Discovery as a paid product**: when scope is genuinely unclear, a paid discovery or audit phase is the professional answer, not a free scoping call disguised as goodwill.
- **The "Ticketmaster clone" trap**: treating a comparison to an existing platform as if it were a scope document — it names an aspiration, not a deliverable list.
- **Estimation boundary language**: having a prepared, calm way to say "not yet" without sounding evasive or unhelpful.

## Example Code
```markdown
## Proposal Readiness Check — [Prospect Name]

**Business problem:** known / unknown
**Desired outcome:** known / unknown
**Must-have scope (first version):** known / partial / unknown
**Users / roles:** known / unknown
**Current system or starting point:** known / unknown
**Timeline expectation:** known / unknown
**Budget signal:** clear / weak / unknown
**Decision-maker / approval process:** known / unknown

**Risk level:** low / medium / high

**Decision:** Ready for proposal / Needs discovery call / Needs intake questionnaire / Not a fit

**Missing information:**
- ...

**Next action:**
- e.g. "Send 8-question intake form before any call is booked."
- e.g. "Offer a paid 2-hour scope workshop (fixed fee) instead of a free discovery call."
```

## When to Use
- Before writing any estimate, price range, or timeline in response to an inbound inquiry.
- When a prospect asks "how much would this cost?" in the first message or call.
- When the described project is a comparison to an existing large platform rather than a specific feature set.
- When there's no named person who can actually approve spending the money.
- Before agreeing to "just send a rough number so we know if we're in the right ballpark" — a rough number still anchors expectations.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating a vivid description ("like Airbnb but for boats") as if it were a scope document.
- Sending a number to avoid an awkward silence, then trying to walk it back later once real scope emerges.
- Skipping the decision-maker question because it feels impolite to ask "who actually approves this?"
- Letting urgency from the prospect ("we need this fast") compress the readiness check instead of triggering a paid discovery phase.
