# 206. Pricing Positioning — Selling Value, Not Hours

## What It Is
The way you talk about your price shapes how it's received almost as much as the number itself. A freelancer who frames their fee as "10 days times my rate" invites the client to mentally negotiate against cheaper labor doing the same arithmetic. A freelancer who frames the same fee as covering requirement clarification, architecture decisions, testing, deployment, documentation, and risk ownership invites the client to evaluate it against the cost of the outcome instead. Pricing positioning is the discipline of consistently choosing the second frame.

Concretely, this means naming the non-coding work explicitly — discovery, UX decisions, error handling, deployment, project management, risk ownership — because clients who've never built software genuinely don't know it's part of the job unless you tell them. It also means never apologizing for a professional price. "Sorry, I know this is expensive, I can lower it" signals that the number was arbitrary in the first place; "if the budget doesn't fit the full version, we can define a smaller MVP — I don't recommend cutting testing or deployment quality" holds the line on quality while still giving the client a real path forward.

Positioning also shows up in what you don't say. Language like "I think this will take about 10 days" or "it's expensive because it's difficult" invites scrutiny of your efficiency or your effort rather than the value delivered. The fix isn't to be cagier about how you work — it's to consistently anchor the conversation on business outcome, delivery risk, and long-term maintainability, which are the things the client is actually buying, whether or not they'd phrase it that way themselves.

## Key Concepts
- **Outcome framing**: pricing communicated around the business result and delivery risk, not screens, pages, or hours.
- **Visible non-coding work**: explicitly naming discovery, UX decisions, testing, deployment, documentation, and project management as part of what the price covers.
- **Price confidence**: never apologizing for a professional price; offering a smaller scope instead of a discount when budget is the real constraint.
- **Weak language to eliminate**: "I think this will take about X days," "normally I charge more but I can do it cheaper," "it's expensive because it's hard" — all of these invite comparison against cheaper labor.
- **Price anchoring via options**: presenting a discovery/workshop tier, an MVP build tier, and an MVP-plus-maintenance tier lets the client choose commitment level instead of facing a single take-it-or-leave-it number.
- **Consistency across documents**: proposal language, verbal framing on calls, and negotiation responses should all use the same value-first vocabulary, not shift into apologetic language under pressure.

## Example Code
```markdown
## Price Framing Checklist — Before Sending a Proposal

Strong framing (use this):
"This price covers full delivery of the scoped first version: planning,
implementation, testing, deployment support, and handover documentation —
not only coding time."

Weak framing (never send this):
"I charge $X/hour and this will take about Y hours, so the total is $Z."

Before sending, verify:
- [ ] Value/outcome is stated before the number appears
- [ ] Non-coding work (testing, deployment, docs, PM) is explicitly listed
- [ ] No apology language appears anywhere near the price
- [ ] A lower-commitment option exists if budget doesn't fit the full scope
- [ ] The price is followed by a question, not a justification
```

## When to Use
- Every time you write the "investment" or "project fee" section of a proposal.
- When a client asks "why does this cost what it does?" — respond with the value list, not a defensive breakdown of hours.
- When you notice yourself about to write "I know this is a lot, but..." — that's the signal to stop and reframe.
- When comparing your price to a cheaper competitor's quote in conversation with the client.

## Common Mistakes
- Presenting price as hourly-rate-times-estimated-hours, which invites the client to shop your efficiency against someone cheaper.
- Apologizing for the price before the client has even reacted to it.
- Describing the price only in terms of screens or pages delivered, ignoring backend, deployment, and risk ownership.
- Offering to "do it cheaper" unprompted, which signals the original number wasn't really the number.
