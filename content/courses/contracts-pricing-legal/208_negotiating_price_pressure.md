# 208. Negotiating Price Pressure — Levers and Live Scripts

## What It Is
> This lesson is general education, not legal advice. The intent is practical judgment — knowing what a clause is for and where the risk sits, not carrying responsibility for drafting or judging one. What actually holds differs by jurisdiction: TR, US, UK, UAE, EU and JP do not treat IP transfer, contractor classification, consumer protection or liability limits the same way, so have anything you sign reviewed where you and your client actually operate.

Negotiation pressure almost always arrives live — on a call, in a meeting, or in a fast back-and-forth message thread — at exactly the moment you have the least time to think. Without a prepared response, the default human reaction is to fill the uncomfortable silence by conceding: lowering the price, adding scope, or promising a faster timeline, none of which were actually asked for in those terms. The single governing rule is simple to state and hard to hold under pressure: never reduce price without changing scope, timeline, payment terms, risk, or commitment in exchange.

The reason this matters commercially is that unstructured discounting doesn't just cost you the discount — it teaches the client that your prices are negotiable fiction, which erodes trust in every number you quote afterward, on this project and the next one. The fix is to always have a named lever ready: reduce scope, split into phases, remove optional features, increase the upfront deposit, extend the timeline, reduce revision rounds, or convert uncertain portions to hourly. When a client asks for a lower price, the professional response identifies which of these levers moves, describes the resulting change concretely, and lets the client decide — rather than silently absorbing the gap in your own margin.

Specific pressure patterns recur often enough to be worth scripting in advance: "that's too expensive," "I got a cheaper quote," "can you do it for [specific lower number]," "we need this faster, same price," and the client who goes quiet after receiving a proposal. Each has a professional, non-confrontational response that holds the position without sounding rigid — the shared structure is to ask a clarifying question first, wait for the real answer, and only then offer a concrete tradeoff. Matching a competitor's price with no scope comparison, or negotiating against yourself by offering concessions before any objection is raised, are the two most common ways this discipline breaks down under pressure.

## Key Concepts
- **The trading rule**: price only moves in exchange for scope, timeline, payment terms, risk, or commitment — never in isolation.
- **Named levers**: reduce scope, split phases, remove optional features, increase deposit, extend timeline, reduce revisions, convert to hourly, or add a maintenance retainer.
- **Ask before conceding**: when a client objects to price, ask what specifically "too expensive" means (total, timing, or budget fit) before offering any change.
- **One concession at a time**: offer a single tradeoff, wait for the reaction, and only consider a second one if genuinely needed — never front-load three concessions in one response.
- **Never negotiate against yourself**: don't proactively lower a price before a client has named a specific objection or number.
- **Written confirmation of every change**: any concession agreed to verbally is followed by a written summary — verbal negotiation outcomes are misremembered by both sides.
- **The stall pattern**: a client who goes quiet after a proposal gets a calm, time-bound follow-up cadence, not daily pings or an unprompted discount.

## Example Code
```markdown
## Live Objection Response — "That's too expensive"

First response:
"I hear you. Can you help me understand what 'too expensive' means here —
is it the total amount, the timing of payments, or does it not fit the
budget you had allocated?"

[Wait for the real answer before offering anything.]

If the issue is total amount:
"The price reflects the full scope we discussed — [name 2–3 key items].
If budget is a firm constraint, the most direct path is reducing the
scope of the first version. I can redesign the proposal around a smaller
core and build the rest in a later phase. Would that be useful?"

Forbidden response:
"I can match their price." — matching without a scope comparison signals
the original number was arbitrary.

## Change Confirmation (always send after any verbal agreement)
"As discussed, here is the revised scope and price: [changes]. Let me
know if this matches your understanding before I update the proposal."
```

## When to Use
- Any live call or meeting where a client pushes back on price, timeline, or scope.
- When a client names a competitor's lower quote as leverage.
- When a proposal goes quiet for more than a few days and needs a structured follow-up, not silence or panic.
- Before any negotiation conversation you know is coming — rehearse the specific script, don't improvise cold.

## Common Mistakes
- **The price is stated, the room goes quiet for a second, and a discount gets offered to fill the silence** — Filling an uncomfortable silence after stating a price by immediately offering a discount.
- **"A competitor quoted less" gets met with "I can match that," before anyone's compared what the two scopes actually include** — Matching a competitor's number without first comparing what's actually included in each scope.
- **The response to "too expensive" offers a lower deposit, fewer revisions, and a longer timeline, all in the same message** — Offering multiple concessions in a single message instead of one at a time.
- **A price change gets agreed on a call, and work continues with nothing written down about it** — Leaving a negotiated change verbal instead of confirming it in writing before proceeding.
