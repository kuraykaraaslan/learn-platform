# 211. Statement of Work (SOW) Structure and Drafting

## What It Is
> This lesson is general education, not legal advice. The intent is practical judgment — knowing what a clause is for and where the risk sits, not carrying responsibility for drafting or judging one. What actually holds differs by jurisdiction: TR, US, UK, UAE, EU and JP do not treat IP transfer, contractor classification, consumer protection or liability limits the same way, so have anything you sign reviewed where you and your client actually operate.

A proposal sells the engagement; the Statement of Work governs it. Once a prospect says yes, the SOW becomes the operational truth of the project — the document a client, a future collaborator, or a court would read to determine what was actually promised. It is not a marketing artifact and should not read like one. Where a proposal can use persuasive language and lead with outcomes, a SOW uses decisive, unambiguous language and leads with control: what's included, what's excluded, who owns which decision, and what happens when something goes wrong.

A complete SOW answers fifteen questions in a predictable order: who the parties are, what the project overview and business objective are, what's included and excluded, what will be delivered, the milestone and payment schedule, what the client must provide, what assumptions and dependencies the estimate rests on, how changes are requested and priced, how many revisions are included, how each milestone is accepted, what happens at handover, and how both sides sign off. Skipping any one of these doesn't just create a gap in the document — it creates a gap in what happens the day a disagreement occurs, because whichever side has the stronger written position wins the disagreement by default.

Language matters more in a SOW than almost anywhere else in the business relationship. "We can probably include that" and "minor additions are fine" are goodwill gestures in a sales call and landmines in a signed document, because a client will reasonably read them as commitments. A SOW should say "this SOW includes... this SOW excludes... any request outside this SOW requires written approval and may affect timeline and price" — plainly, every time, even when it feels blunt in the moment.

This is educational material on how to structure a SOW, not a legal template ready to sign. Contract law, consumer protection rules, and enforceability of specific clauses vary by jurisdiction and by client type (consumer vs. business) — have your actual SOW template reviewed by a lawyer qualified where you and your client operate before you rely on it commercially.

## Key Concepts
- **Contract stack**: Proposal → SOW/Scope Document → Commercial Terms → Contract/Agreement → Change Request Template → Acceptance Sign-off → Handover Record — each document has one job, and when they conflict, the SOW/contract should state which one wins.
- **The fifteen-section structure**: parties, overview, objective, included scope, excluded scope, deliverables, milestones/timeline, payment terms, client responsibilities, assumptions/dependencies, change request process, revision policy, acceptance criteria, handover/support, sign-off.
- **Decisive language rule**: "this SOW includes / excludes," "work begins after deposit and written approval," "any request outside this SOW requires written approval" — replacing soft, hedging language that reads as a promise later.
- **Acceptance clause**: defines what counts as milestone submission, the feedback window, and what happens if no blocking issue is reported in that window.
- **Change clause**: states upfront, before any dispute exists, that workflow changes, new features, new integrations, or rework of accepted deliverables are handled as change requests.

## Example Code
```markdown
# Statement of Work: [Project Name]

## 1. Parties
Client: [Company/Individual]        Contractor: [Your business name]

## 2. Project Overview
[2–3 sentence summary of what is being built]

## 3. Business Objective
[The outcome this project exists to produce]

## 4. Included Scope
[Modules and workflows — see scope-definition lesson]

## 5. Excluded Scope
[Explicit list]

## 6. Deliverables
[Concrete, inspectable outputs]

## 7. Milestones and Timeline
| Milestone | Deliverable | Payment Gate |
|---|---|---|

## 8. Payment Terms
[Deposit / milestone / final — see payment-gates lesson]

## 9. Client Responsibilities
[Content, access, approvals — see client-responsibilities lesson]

## 10. Assumptions and Dependencies
[Conditions this estimate depends on]

## 11. Change Request Process
Any request that changes agreed workflows, adds features, changes design
direction after approval, or requires rework of accepted deliverables will
be handled as a change request affecting cost and/or timeline.

## 12. Revision Policy
[Number, type, and deadline of included revisions]

## 13. Acceptance Criteria
A milestone is submitted when deliverables are made available via demo,
staging link, or document. Client provides consolidated written feedback
within [X] business days. No blocking issue reported in that window may be
treated as acceptance for scheduling and payment purposes.

## 14. Handover and Support
[What's included at final delivery; warranty period — see warranty lesson]

## 15. Sign-off
Client: _______________  Date: _______
Contractor: _______________  Date: _______
```

## When to Use
- Every time a proposal is accepted and before any implementation work begins.
- When converting a discovery call or informal agreement into an execution-ready document.
- Any time a client, PM, or future maintainer needs a single source of truth for what was agreed.

## Common Mistakes
- **The SOW is the proposal's persuasive copy, pasted in with the price updated** — Reusing proposal language (persuasive, outcome-focused) directly in the SOW instead of rewriting it as control language.
- **"We're on good terms, we don't need to be that formal about it" is why the SOW skips exclusions and payment gates** — Leaving out exclusions, payment gates, or an acceptance process because "the relationship is friendly, we don't need to be formal."
- **The SOW says one thing about revisions and the commercial terms say another, with no note on which one wins** — Letting the SOW and the underlying contract/commercial terms silently contradict each other with no stated precedence.
- **The SOW gets signed and filed away, and nobody reopens it when the first change request comes in** — Treating the SOW as a one-time document instead of the reference both sides return to when a change request or dispute arises.
