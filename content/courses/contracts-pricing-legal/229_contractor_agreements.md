# 229. Contractor Agreements — Protecting Yourself When You Hire Help

## What It Is
> This lesson is general education, not legal advice. The intent is practical judgment — knowing what a clause is for and where the risk sits, not carrying responsibility for drafting or judging one. What actually holds differs by jurisdiction: TR, US, UK, UAE, EU and JP do not treat IP transfer, contractor classification, consumer protection or liability limits the same way, so have anything you sign reviewed where you and your client actually operate.

Every clause this course has covered so far protects a freelancer as the party being hired. The moment a solo practice grows enough to bring in a subcontractor — a designer, a second developer, an overseas specialist — the freelancer becomes the hiring party, and needs the mirror image of the same protections, this time pointed the other direction. A contractor agreement is not a formality for that first small engagement; it's the document that determines whether the freelancer actually owns what the subcontractor produces, and whether they have any real recourse if something goes wrong.

The clauses that matter most are the same ones a good client-facing contract has, restated from the hiring side: clear parties and scope, so there's no ambiguity about what's being delivered; an explicit IP assignment clause, because in most legal systems a subcontractor's payment does not automatically transfer ownership of what they wrote — without an explicit clause, they can in principle retain rights to code the freelancer has already delivered to their own client; a confidentiality clause with a bounded duration; clearly stated payment terms tied to milestones and documentation; an independent-contractor status clause that describes the relationship accurately (the subcontractor sets their own hours and methods, uses their own tools) rather than one that reads like disguised employment; concrete, auditable deliverables and an acceptance process; and a termination clause covering notice period and file handover.

A handful of specific danger signs in a subcontractor's own proposed agreement are worth learning to spot immediately: no IP assignment clause at all; the word "license" where "assignment" was needed, which lets the subcontractor keep the underlying ownership; payment on invoice only with no milestone structure, which removes all leverage after delivery; no defined acceptance process, which lets a dispute about "was this actually delivered" run forever; and any clause claiming rights to use the freelancer's own client's name or logo, which isn't the subcontractor's relationship to claim. None of the specific clause language in this lesson is a ready-to-sign template — contractor agreements sit exactly at the intersection of IP law, tax classification, and (in many places) labor law, and the cost of a bad one shows up much later than the cost of a lawyer's review upfront.

## Key Concepts
- **Mirror-image protection**: the same commercial protections a freelancer wants from a client (written scope, IP assignment, payment gates, termination terms) apply in reverse when the freelancer is the one hiring.
- **Mandatory clause set**: parties and scope, IP assignment, confidentiality, payment terms, independent-contractor status, deliverables and acceptance, termination, and governing law — treated as non-negotiable before work starts, not added later.
- **IP assignment is not automatic**: in most jurisdictions a subcontractor's payment alone does not transfer code ownership; without an explicit written clause, the freelancer cannot guarantee clean IP to their own client.
- **Independent-contractor status clause**: language describing the relationship accurately — the subcontractor controls their own hours, tools, and methods — reduces the risk of the relationship being reclassified as employment later.
- **Red flags in a subcontractor's own agreement**: missing IP assignment, "license" instead of "assignment," invoice-only payment with no milestones, no acceptance process, and any claim to use the freelancer's client's name or brand.

## Example Code
```template
## Contractor Agreement — Mandatory Clauses (illustrative)

**Parties & Scope:** [Full legal names]. Subject: delivery of [specific
deliverable] for the [project name] engagement.

**IP Assignment:** Contractor assigns to [Your Business] all right, title,
and interest in work product created under this agreement, effective upon
payment. Contractor retains no license, royalty, or reuse claim over the
delivered work.

**Confidentiality:** Contractor will keep confidential all client names,
project details, technical infrastructure, and business information
learned under this agreement, for [3] years following its end.

**Payment:** [Amount], split across [milestones], payable within [X]
business days of accepted deliverable and required documentation.

**Independent Contractor Status:** No employment relationship is created.
Contractor determines their own working hours, tools, and methods.

**Deliverables & Acceptance:** [Specific, auditable list]. Deliverable is
accepted if no written objection is raised within [X] business days.

**Termination:** Either party may terminate with [7] days' written notice.
Payment is due for completed work; contractor delivers all in-progress
files within [2] business days of termination.

**Governing Law:** [Jurisdiction], with disputes resolved in [forum].
```

## When to Use
- Before any subcontractor, freelancer, or agency partner starts work on your behalf.
- When reviewing an agreement a subcontractor has proposed to you, rather than one you've drafted yourself.
- Whenever you're about to pass a subcontractor's deliverable through to your own client and need to confirm the IP chain is unbroken.

## Common Mistakes
- **A subcontractor starts work on a handshake, "because they come well recommended," with nothing signed yet** — Letting a subcontractor start work on a verbal understanding "because they seem trustworthy," with no signed agreement at all.
- **The subcontractor's own contract template gets signed as-is, with nobody checking whether it actually assigns IP or just licenses it** — Accepting a subcontractor's own proposed contract without checking for a missing or watered-down IP clause.
- **The full fee gets paid upfront to a new subcontractor before a single deliverable has shown up** — Paying 100% upfront with no milestone structure, removing all leverage if delivery is late or incomplete.
- **The "independent contractor" agreement requires fixed hours, exclusive availability, and the hiring party's own tools and instructions** — Writing a status clause that describes something close to employment (fixed hours, exclusive engagement, your tools and instructions) while calling it a contractor relationship.
