# 231. Paying Contractors — Structure, Documentation, and Leverage

## What It Is
Paying a subcontractor well is not just about fairness — it's about sequencing and documentation, and getting the sequence wrong creates real exposure for the hiring party specifically. The order that protects both sides is fixed: the contractor delivers, the hiring party formally accepts in writing, the contractor issues the tax/invoicing document their jurisdiction requires, and only then does payment go out. Paying before receiving that document trades a small amount of friction now for a real, hard-to-fix problem later — in most tax systems, an expense paid without proper documentation isn't deductible and can draw scrutiny in an audit, entirely separate from whether the underlying work was legitimate.

Milestone structure matters as much on the paying side as it does on the receiving side covered earlier in this course, and the same principle transfers directly: never pay 100% upfront. A short fixed-price engagement might run 30–50% upfront with the balance on delivery; a longer one splits across a deposit, one or more milestones, and a final payment; hourly or weekly work bills on a regular cadence rather than in one lump sum. Paying everything upfront removes every incentive for a subcontractor to prioritize quality or timeliness, because there's nothing left to protect once the money has moved.

Two habits round out a clean payment process: define upfront whether project-related expenses (software licenses, cloud costs, tools) are included in the fixed price or reimbursed separately against receipts, and keep a complete record for every payment — the transfer confirmation, the invoicing/tax document, and the written acceptance that triggered the payment — filed together, not scattered across email. The specific tax document types, withholding requirements, and record-retention periods mentioned in source material for this lesson are jurisdiction-specific (and can differ significantly even between two contractors in different countries) — treat the sequencing and structure here as the universal principle, and confirm the specific tax and documentation requirements for your situation with an accountant.

## Key Concepts
- **Fixed payment sequence**: delivery → written acceptance → invoicing/tax document received → payment released — never reordered, even under time pressure.
- **Documentation-before-payment principle**: paying without the required tax/invoicing document creates audit exposure and removes proof of a legitimate business expense, regardless of whether the work itself was legitimate.
- **Never-100%-upfront rule**: milestone or periodic payment structures preserve leverage for quality and timeliness throughout the engagement, mirroring the same principle covered for client-facing payment terms.
- **Expense reimbursement clarity**: stated upfront whether project expenses are bundled into the fixed price or reimbursed separately against receipts, with a cap if open-ended.
- **Complete payment records**: transfer confirmation, invoicing/tax document, and written acceptance filed together for every payment, retained for as long as local rules require.

## Example Code
```markdown
## Contractor Payment Sequence

1. Contractor delivers the agreed work product.
2. [Hiring Business] formally accepts in writing (a brief email confirming
   acceptance is sufficient).
3. Contractor issues the required invoicing/tax document.
4. Payment is released within [X] business days of receiving that document.

## Milestone Structure by Engagement Type
| Engagement Type | Upfront | Mid-point | On Delivery |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Short fixed-price (<3 months) | 30–50% | — | 50–70% |
| Longer fixed-price (>3 months) | 30% | 40% per milestone | 30% |
| Hourly/weekly | — | Billed weekly/bi-weekly | — |

## Payment Record Checklist (per payment)
- [ ] Bank transfer or payment confirmation
- [ ] Invoicing/tax document received and verified
- [ ] Written acceptance of the deliverable that triggered payment
- [ ] Filed together in one record, not scattered across email/chat
```

## When to Use
- Every time a subcontractor completes a milestone or deliverable that triggers payment.
- When structuring the payment schedule for a new subcontractor engagement.
- When deciding whether a project-related expense is reimbursable and under what documentation standard.

## Common Mistakes
- Paying a subcontractor before receiving the invoicing/tax document their jurisdiction requires, purely to keep the relationship friendly.
- Paying 100% upfront for a new or unproven subcontractor relationship, removing all leverage if the work is late or substandard.
- Reimbursing expenses without requiring receipts, which is both hard to justify later and often not tax-deductible.
- Keeping payment records scattered across email and chat instead of filed together per contractor, per engagement.
