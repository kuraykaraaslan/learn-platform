# Judge 03 — Credibility / anti-slop

> Verbatim output of the `judge:credibility` agent. It received all six audits and all 32 proposals,
> and scored every proposal it considered worth keeping under this lens alone.

## Judging lens

CREDIBILITY: does this make the corpus more defensible to a skeptical senior reader, or does it multiply plausible-sounding generated text? I re-verified the load-bearing audit claims in the repo before scoring rather than trusting them: 412 lessons confirmed; Further Reading bullet distribution is 390 files at exactly 3, 16 at 4, 6 at 2, zero at 1 or 5+ (a hard quota with zero variance — the most legible generation signature in the product); 0 of 412 lessons contain a code fence inside Common Mistakes; only 132 of 412 have any URL in Further Reading; the fabricated "The Business of Software — Michael Feathers and Patrick McKenzie" citation is real and verbatim at process-soft-skills/83:150; the dead cognitect.com ADR link is real at 75:156; 5 files carry date-suffixed model IDs the API rejects; 27 lesson files import `@/libs/*`, `@/modules/*` or `@/stores/*` while `libs/` in this repo contains only `icons.ts` and `utils`; 34 files carry audit residue and 20 leak owner identity; the bracket-before-id sort bug is real at course_content.service.ts:77. I also confirmed proposal 25's parser claim precisely — `if (currentField) buffer.push(line)` drops every pre-heading line, so frontmatter is render-invisible, and the fence branch pushes unconditionally, which is a latent collapse bug worth nailing down before 412 files gain a header. Zustand is a declared dependency with zero imports; vitest is wired with zero test files.

Given that, my ordering principle is blunt: this corpus's problem is not that it is thin, it is that nothing in the repo can tell you which claims are false, and it has already been caught shipping invented citations, uncompilable code and wrong security mitigations. Adding 412 lessons' worth of new generated prose on that foundation makes the product worse, not better — a skeptical reader who catches one fabrication discounts all 412. So I rank verification machinery, primary-source plumbing, human-authored anchors and deletion passes above every content-authoring idea, and I rank content-authoring ideas by whether they produce a checkable artifact (a passing CI run, a resolving URL, a statute number, a real terminal output) or merely more confident sentences.

MERGES: #12 (Broken→Fixed→Proof) merged into #28 (content/_verify) — 12 is the content shape, 28 is the enforcement, and shipping either alone wastes the other; #28 also absorbs #2's useful half (the CI check that the accepted answer substituted back into the snippet compiles). #3 (When It Breaks) and #10 (How It Breaks) are the same proposal from two lenses — merged under #10. #9 (Depth Tiers) merged into #25 (Frontmatter Spine): `depth` is one key of that schema and both touch the identical three files. #18's two-line sort fix is folded into #30 as its stated prerequisite (it is a correctness defect today, not a feature).

NOT SCORED, NOT REJECTED — neutral on this lens: #5 Return Queue, #6/#23 Capstones (merged; 23 human-authored units is a small fabrication surface and a real transfer artifact, but it does not make any existing claim more checkable), #7/#17 concept and prereq graph (repairing 127 dead `#N` refs is a genuine sloppiness fix at zero generated-text cost — worth doing, just not a trust mechanism), #13 Decide, #20 search, #21 Failure Drill (reuses existing bullets, near-zero fabrication risk), #22 Course Pack. These are navigation and pedagogy work. They make an unfixed corpus easier to move around in, which is the wrong first move but not a harmful one.

### Reasoning, in ranked order

#### 9.5 — content/_verify — the Examples Become a Repo That Runs (#28, merged with #12 Broken → Fixed → Proof and #2's compile gate)

The only proposal in all 32 where the corpus lying about itself turns CI red. I verified its premise directly: 27 lesson files import `@/libs/logger`-style private aliases while this repo's `libs/` holds only `icons.ts` and `utils`, so those snippets cannot resolve for anyone; 5 files hardcode a date-suffixed model ID the API rejects, meaning a reader's very first attempt to USE the corpus fails. Extracting fences into a real workspace and injecting actual pass/fail output between machine-owned markers replaces the corpus's weakest evidence (a decorative fence nobody ran) with its strongest (a dated, reproducible run). I merge #12 into it because #12 supplies the shape — broken version, symptom, fix, proof — and #28 supplies the only thing that keeps that shape honest; #12 alone would be three times the generated text with no verifier. Its incidental finding is the sharpest in the set: a fence inside `## Common Mistakes` already parses and renders correctly today (I confirmed the fence tracker is section-agnostic and that 0 of 412 lessons use this), so the single highest-value credibility addition needs no parser change. XL cost is real and confined to ~175 technical lessons, which is correct — forcing a verify dir onto a business lesson is theater.

#### 9.3 — One Source Ledger, Cited by Slug (#29)

Citation fabrication is the acute trust failure here, and I confirmed it verbatim rather than taking the audit's word: the Feathers/McKenzie attribution sits at process-soft-skills/83:150, the phantom OWASP screenshot-sanitizing doc at 310:74, the mistitled Evan Miller piece at 93:171, and the dead cognitect.com link at 75:156. Only 132 of 412 lessons carry any URL in Further Reading at all. A ledger where a citation must resolve to a key some named human dated makes 'Michael Feathers and Patrick McKenzie' structurally unable to enter the corpus, and the build-time throw on an unresolved slug means it cannot creep back in. Two things earn it the near-top score under my lens specifically: the `why` line converts a bibliography into a route to depth rather than a gesture at it, and the triage is explicitly deletion-first — deleting ~40 unverifiable bullets is worth more than rescuing 400, because one caught fabrication invalidates the other 1,245. The weekly HEAD-check job is the part that keeps it true after launch. Correctly flags that this pass cannot be delegated to generation: a model asked to find a missing URL will invent one for a book that does not exist.

#### 9 — Provenance and the Human Gate (#31)

This is the direct structural answer to my lens's central warning — that 412 machine-enriched lessons can be worse than 412 lean ones. The `body_sha` rule makes 'an LLM edited this and nobody read it' a build failure rather than an intention, and `HUMAN_ONLY.md` is the single highest-leverage document proposed anywhere in these 32: Turkish jurisdictional content, security mitigations, any number a reader will quote in a negotiation, and the ambiguous-outcome paragraph are precisely the places where a fluent generated answer is indistinguishable from a correct one to everyone except the person harmed by it. The lesson-34 example proves the point — the corpus's current wrong constant-time answer is exactly what a model would produce again. Versioned prompts under git also give the owner the ability to detect and re-run a poisoned batch instead of re-reading 412 files, which is the only affordable recovery path at this scale. Docked slightly because process weight is a genuine failure mode; the mitigation (gate applies only at the verified transition, drafts flow freely and are simply unbadged) is the right one.

#### 8.8 — content:lint — Fourteen Rules and One Waiver File (#26)

Converts every audit finding from a one-time observation into a build invariant, which matters because consistency achieved by authoring discipline decays across batches and consistency enforced by CI does not. Its most interesting rule is validated by my own measurement: `sources/quota-signature` fires on zero-variance Further Reading counts, and the real distribution is 390 files at exactly 3, 16 at 4, 6 at 2 — no lesson anywhere has 1 or 5+. That is not a style issue, it is the corpus telling a skeptical reader it was generated to a template. `sources/bare-domain` is equally well-grounded (150 bullets that look sourced and render as grey text under this repo's own remark-gfm pipeline), and `code/fence-lang` fixes 226 template lessons currently rendering `##` and `**` as monospace. The warn-for-one-wave-then-promote discipline and the expiring waiver file are what stop this from becoming bypassed ceremony. One rule is genuinely dangerous — `voice/asserted-second-person`, given that 93.6% of the corpus's 2,784 you/your tokens are legitimate instructional address — and the proposal says so, which is why I trust the rest of it.

#### 8.5 — Six Gold Standards and a Falsifiable Rubric (#27)

Human-authored anchors are the thing my lens rewards most after verification, and this is the only proposal that produces them deliberately. The load-bearing design decision is that dimensions 1, 2 and 5 are pass/fail on artifacts — a passing CI run, a fence inside Common Mistakes, a named ambiguous-outcome case — so a score cannot be talked up by adding words, which is exactly how every rubric of this kind normally rots into a filler checklist. Keeping the golds in `content/_standards/` rather than `content/courses/` is a small but correct call: they anchor without being graded by their own rubric. The eight dimensions are also the most honest operational definition of the owner's goal anyone has offered here — they name what a reader can do after a good lesson and cannot do after a glossary. The stated risk is the real one and should be accepted rather than mitigated: six hand-written gold lessons is a week of the owner's own writing, and delegating that week defeats the entire purpose of the exercise.

#### 8.3 — Jurisdiction Notes (#16)

The only proposal here that fixes content which is actively harmful rather than merely thin. A Turkish reader following contracts-pricing-legal literally invoices without e-Fatura, is taught the IR35 employment test instead of the SGK/muvazaa exposure that actually applies to them, and never learns they hold statutory default interest they have never invoiced. Under a credibility lens this is the sharpest possible win, because being handed the instrument — the statute number, the publisher of the rate, the filing obligation — is categorically different from being handed a summary of it, and it is verifiable by a reviewer opening mevzuat.gov.tr rather than by judging prose. The staleness rule is the best-engineered detail in the depth cluster: name the publisher, the instrument and the month it is published, never the rate itself, so the sentence survives annual revision without an annual rewrite. Moving the 40 stamped disclaimers out of Further Reading (where they currently consume a reference slot in lessons that have zero real links) is a clean secondary fix. Docked only because it carries real liability and cannot ship without a mali müşavir and a lawyer pass — but that cost is the point, not an objection.

#### 8 — Numbers That Matter (#11)

Changes the corpus's mood from assertion to evidence in the place a senior reader tests first — a trade-off defended with an adjective is not defended. The enforcement rule is what makes it credible rather than decorative: every row either cites the documentation that publishes the number inline or gives the command that produces it, and a row with neither is deleted. That is a reviewable artifact, not a judgment of writing quality, which is the only review that stays honest across 412 lessons. Two details raise it above the rest of the depth cluster: stamping the version in the column header ('PostgreSQL 16 default', never 'default'), and preferring the measurement command over the stated value wherever both exist — a command never goes stale, which makes this the most rot-resistant content proposal in the 32. The explicit ban on derived performance claims unless the lesson ships the benchmark is exactly right; an unsourced 'SERIALIZABLE costs 15%' is worse than the adjective it replaced, because it invites a reader to repeat it in a design review. Also seeds the corpus's first real inline citations, of which there are currently 6.

#### 7.8 — The Frontmatter Spine (#25, merged with #9 Depth Tiers)

Low glamour, hard prerequisite — most of what I ranked above it needs a schema to write into and a snapshot test to make 412-file mechanical passes survivable. I verified its two technical claims exactly: `if (currentField) buffer.push(line)` drops every line before the first recognized `##`, so frontmatter lands with a byte-identical render, and the fence branch does push unconditionally, so a stray ``` in an intro would flip `inFence`, swallow every later heading and collapse a lesson into one section. Zero files trigger that today, which is precisely why it should be nailed down before 412 files gain a header. The strictly-ordered mitigation — snapshot test on the current corpus first, then the parser change, then the frontmatter, snapshots must not move at either step — is the discipline that separates this from a risky mass edit, and it lands this repo's first test file against a parser that currently has none. I merge #9 into it because `depth` is one key of this schema and both touch the identical three files; #9's independent contribution is its anti-padding CI rule (a deep lesson whose What It Is exceeds 400 words fails), which should survive the merge. Generated manifests also end the second-copy-of-the-truth problem.

#### 7.5 — The Freshness Contract (#32)

Dating is what separates a claim a reader will act on from background reading, and acting on it is where the owner's goal actually gets met. It catches defects I confirmed exist: the cognitect.com link that goes nowhere, four retired nextjs.org doc paths in a Next.js 15 repo, and 5 lessons handing the reader a model ID the API rejects — so a reader's first attempt to use the corpus fails, teaching them it is decorative. The single most important constraint is stated correctly: the verified line is machine-written only, fed strictly from frontmatter, rendered only when CI actually verified it, never hand-settable. A badge that lies is worse than no badge, and most products get this wrong. The per-course verified count on the overview page is quietly the most honest thing in these 32 proposals — it shows a buyer real coverage instead of implying uniform quality. The 'if quarterly review on the ~25 AI/LLM lessons is unaffordable, show the old date rather than pretend' fallback is the right instinct, and S effort makes it nearly free once #25 and #29 exist.

#### 7.2 — The First 40: a Computed Queue, Batched by Concept (#30, with #18's sort fix as prerequisite)

Sequencing is a credibility decision, not a project-management one: harm × entry-point ordering means the 12 actively harmful lessons (SSRF, timing, JWT, saga, MVCC, zero-downtime migration, the OTel PII-in-span, contractor classification, tax readiness) get fixed before a single tail lesson is enriched, and the first two lessons a reader opens in any course are the enriched ones — which is where judgment of the whole product forms. Batching by concept rather than by course is the non-obvious right call and it is well argued: a course-shaped batch decides 'retry' terminology 14 separate times, differently. The rule that earns most of this score is the Wave 3 constraint — additive only, enforced by a diff check rejecting deletion of existing prose lines — which is the single most direct guardrail against exactly the outcome my lens fears, the tail being quietly rewritten into uniform AI voice. Cutting rather than generating over the four memo-lessons (100, 114, 85, 98) is correct; those are consulting memos to the first owner, not lessons. Fold in #18's two-line sort fix, which I confirmed at course_content.service.ts:77, since the queue's entry-point term is meaningless until it lands. S effort, and it inherits its scores' errors — hand-audit the top 10 rows before Wave 1.

#### 6.8 — Field Notes (#14)

The highest reward and the highest fabrication risk in the entire set, and my score is a genuine split. On reward: an abstraction fused to one dated, sourced, real incident is the most credible thing a lesson can contain, and the third beat — an honest audit of which controls in this very lesson would NOT have stopped it — is a move nothing else in these 32 makes. It signals that the author is grading his own work rather than selling it, which is precisely the posture that earns a skeptical reader's trust in the other 411 lessons. On risk: this is the proposal an AI pass will most confidently fake, and a plausible invented incident in a security lesson would end the product's credibility permanently — the corpus has already been caught with roughly seven invented citations, so this is a demonstrated failure mode, not a hypothetical. Viable only strictly downstream of #29's ledger, with the stated hard rule enforced mechanically (every Field Note links a primary document; human review opens the link; no public incident means the section is omitted). Prefer the vendor's own postmortem or a court filing over journalism — retelling a famous incident wrongly is nearly as damaging as inventing one. 100 sourced entries beat 200 with five inventions.

#### 6.3 — How It Breaks (#10, merged with #3 When It Breaks)

Same proposal from two lenses, so I merge them; both target the corpus's real operational void, which I take seriously — 0 of 412 files contain 'you should see' or 'how to verify', so nothing a reader does can currently confirm they understood. Real diagnostic output plus the knob that bounds the failure is a strong trust artifact, and recognizing a `wait_event` pattern three years later is a genuinely different form of knowing than recalling a prose warning. But it scores lowest of what I would keep because hallucinated psql output is visually indistinguishable from real psql output, and this section would sit in the lessons where a wrong answer costs most — the corpus already ships confidently wrong mitigations inside Common Mistakes, which is the least likely place a reader questions them. Fundable only bound to #28's harness: every fenced output pasted from an actual run, a reproduction committed under `content/repro/<lesson-id>/`, and omission rather than invention when the author cannot produce one. That last rule must be mechanical (no repro dir, no section) rather than an intention, because intentions do not survive wave 3. Cap at 3 entries; a section of exotic trivia is worse than nothing.

## Rejected (6)

- Close the Tab (#4) — ~1,650 generated recall items across 412 lessons, self-graded against generated checklists, with no verification hook anywhere: pure unverified text multiplication whose 'must have mentioned' criteria would silently canonize whatever the lesson already got wrong.

- Cold Open (#1) — puts 412 generated multiple-choice items and ~1,650 generated distractor rationales as the first thing on every page, in a corpus where 5 of 13 sampled technical lessons contain wrong code; the feedback layer would inherit and amplify errors at the exact position a skeptical reader forms their judgment. Reconsider only after #27 and #28 have landed.

- Prove It (#15) — invents what a senior 'would ask' plus the version-specific trivia that answers it (error codes, log message ids, annotation behavior) with zero verification anchor; this is the precise shape of fluent confabulation the corpus has already been caught producing, dressed as expertise.

- Two Doors (#8) — 30-40 generated drill files whose value rests entirely on answer keys that its own author concedes are 'genuinely arguable'; a contestable answer key presented as authoritative destroys credibility faster than a thin lesson does.

- Türkçe Katman (#24) — doubles 412 files to 824 with an acknowledged stale-translation hazard the author himself calls worse than no Turkish at all; #16 delivers the entire jurisdictional correctness win without doubling the maintenance surface or the fabrication surface.

- Portable Progress (#19) — ticks, streaks and an export file change nothing about whether a single claim in the corpus is true; harmless in itself, but building it before the verification layer tells a buyer the product is about completion rather than correctness.
